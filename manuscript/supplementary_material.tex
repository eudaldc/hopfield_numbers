\section{Supplementary Material 1: Trial-level fitting}
\label{sm:methods}

We reconstructed one row per trial from the openly available data of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}. For each trial, we retained the presented numerosity, typed response, exposure duration, and response time. The sample size reported in the source article and the number of sessions in the open file refer to different samples. The article analysed four between-experiment arms with $N=100$ in each arm, after excluding the ten highest-error participants per arm. The \texttt{DURATION} file used here instead pools the three duration-control arms (\texttt{NONE}, \texttt{AREA}, and \texttt{DENSITY}) without that exclusion. It contains 379 unique session identifiers that contributed at least one duration trial; 360 of these sessions contain data at all five exposure durations. Across the five durations (40, 80, 160, 320, and 640~ms) and numerosities $n=1,\ldots,15$, the reconstructed dataset contains 52,995 trials, approximately 10,600 per duration. All likelihoods are evaluated on the individual integer reports; no trials are aggregated into summary curves.

The model starts with the completed sensory estimate $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(n,\,\sigma(n)^2\big)$, with $\sigma(n)=\sqrt{s_{00}^2 + s_0\,n^{\lambda_0}}$ and fixed baseline $s_{00}=0.05$; only $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ are fitted (per duration in the joint fit). Integer memory components sit at $m=1,\ldots,19$ (the true range $1$--$15$ plus four units of padding), and the readout softmax and the energy $V$ are defined over this same support. Typed responses run up to $R_{\max}=99$; a few per cent fall outside the well support ($3.9$--$6.1\%$ across durations) and are handled by the lapse channel below.

From $x=y_0$ the state follows the discrete descent $x\leftarrow x-\mathrm{clip}(\eta\,\partial V/\partial x,\,\pm\tfrac12\min_m\sigma_m)$ with fixed step size $\eta=1$ and global clamp $\tfrac12\min_m\sigma_m$ (equal to $\tfrac12\sigma_c$ for the power-law wells).
% OLD: Descent stops at the first iteration $t$ for which either $|\mathrm{step}|<\mathrm{tol}$ with $\mathrm{tol}=10^{-4}$, or the step reverses sign relative to the previous update (oscillation criterion: $\mathrm{step}_t\cdot\mathrm{step}_{t-1}<0$).
Descent stops at the first zero-based iteration $t$ for which either $|\mathrm{step}|<\mathrm{tol}$ with $\mathrm{tol}=10^{-4}$, or the step reverses sign relative to the previous update (oscillation criterion: $\mathrm{step}_t\cdot\mathrm{step}_{t-1}<0$).
% OLD: In both cases the reversing or sub-threshold step is \emph{not} taken, so the resting state $x^*$ is the state at detection and the settling time is $T=t$.
In both cases the reversing or sub-threshold step is \emph{not} taken, so the resting state $x^*$ is the state at detection; the stopping iteration is nevertheless counted, giving the recorded count $T=t+1$ under the code's zero-based index.
On the flat large-numerosity landscape, some trajectories do not meet the numerical settling criterion within an iteration budget compatible with the observed response times. Since reaching the eventual settling point can imply implausibly long predicted response times, we stop clean-up after $G$ steps.
% OLD: Profiling over all possible configurations places the optimum at $G=120$ ($\Delta\log L=0$), which we use in the clean-up model.
Across the tested budget grid, $G\in\{40,60,100,120,140,160,250\}$, the response likelihood was highest at $G=120$ ($\Delta\log L=0$), which we use in the clean-up model.

For each model and duration the response probability of Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:marginal}) marginalizes the readout over the sensory channel. The integral is computed deterministically, so the objective is free of Monte-Carlo noise. Gauss--Hermite quadrature is inappropriate here: for the attractor models the clean-up map $y_0 \mapsto x^*$ is near-discontinuous (evidence snaps to basins), and Gauss--Hermite carries a $1.7$--$4\%$ error even at $241$ nodes. We therefore use a uniform standard-normal grid on $z\in[-7,7]$ with Gaussian weights for every model, so the comparison is on identical footing. Against high-sample Monte-Carlo estimates, a grid of $801$ nodes matches the integral to $\le 2\times10^{-3}$ (mean $1.5\times10^{-4}$). The optimizer uses a cheaper grid ($201$ nodes); the reported log-likelihoods are recomputed at the fitted parameters on a fine grid ($1601$ nodes).

We never discard out-of-support responses: the final response probability is $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$ with $R_{\max} = 99$, a uniform contamination over $r=1,\ldots,99$ that places positive mass everywhere. The lapse is counted in every AIC and BIC. It is fitted separately at each duration in the attractor, static-readout, Weber/ANS, and C\&P fits. The published-form joint C\&P fit instead shares one lapse across durations; we therefore also fit a matched joint C\&P variant with duration-specific lapses.

% OLD: Response time is modelled as log-normal, $\log \mathrm{rt} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(t_0 + m(n)), \sigma_{\mathrm{rt}})$, so main-text Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:rt}) is the location of this log-normal (the predicted mean RT).
Response time is modelled as log-normal, $\log \mathrm{rt} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(t_0 + m(n)), \sigma_{\mathrm{rt}})$, so main-text Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:rt}) is its location on the response-time scale---the predicted median and geometric mean, not the arithmetic mean.
The metric $m(n)$ is the expected posterior entropy ($\kappa\,\mathbb{E}[H]$), the expected convergence time ($\tau\,\mathbb{E}[T]$), or their sum (the hybrid). We fix the basal latency at $t_0=1.30$~s: because RT is timed from stimulus offset, this intercept absorbs mask-viewing, typing, and motor time, and at the longest exposures the geometric-mean RT for $n=1$ approaches ${\sim}1.30$~s, placing $t_0$ at the floor of the observed chronometry. It is held fixed for all models and durations and is not estimated from the likelihood. Link coefficients are shared across durations and residual scatter $\sigma_{\mathrm{rt}}$ is profiled per duration. The steps-only link is selected by AIC, narrowly ahead of the hybrid ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=0.24$) and far ahead of entropy alone ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=1579.5$). All three are fitted with the landscape held fixed at its response-fit values, providing a constrained cross-channel test of the fitted landscape.

The deterministic objective is minimized by differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} followed by a Nelder--Mead polish, with fixed seeds.

\section{Supplementary Material 2: Model comparison and constrained fits}
\label{sm:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} shows the joint response-model fits and states the sharing configuration used for every row. In the power-law attractor and static fits, only the landscape parameters $(\sigma_c,p)$ are shared; in the constant-width ablations, $p=0$ is fixed and only $\sigma_c$ is shared. The sensory parameters $(s_0,\lambda_0)$ and lapse are duration-specific. The Weber/ANS model has no separable landscape, so its four parameters remain duration-specific.

The normative benchmark is the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, reimplemented from their published code: a Zipf ($1/n^2$) prior read out through an information-bottleneck posterior whose information budget is $\min(B, R\,t)$ bits, with $t$ the exposure duration. Its published joint form shares $B$, $R$, and one lapse across durations ($k=3$). Because the other joint models use duration-specific lapses, the matched C\&P comparison shares only $B$ and $R$ and fits five lapses ($k=7$); this is the C\&P row used in main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}a. Our implementation reproduces the published parameters on simulated data and matches the reference likelihood to machine precision.

Turning the clean-up dynamics on adds a single parameter, the shared give-up budget $G$ (\hyperref[sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}); the dynamics are otherwise deterministic given the landscape. Even net of this penalty, the attractor improves AIC by $721.6$ over its matched static readout in the joint fit. Fixing $p=0$, and hence $\sigma_n=\sigma_c$, worsens AIC by $3488.2$ (both models share the budget, so this comparison is unaffected). Clean-up therefore remains beneficial under the ablation, but it cannot compensate for removing magnitude-dependent broadening.

We also explored a further alternative that tries to model both the accumulation of numerical sensory input and inference as the same mechanism. This means that we dispense with the completed estimate altogether, so that the items are registered one at a time over the exposure and the running count itself drives the attractor, while the landscape pulls the state towards the stored integers (main-text Methods), with the response read out at offset. This version fits notably worse than the completed-estimate attractor (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}), providing evidence for a two-step process of numerical perception: accumulation and then readout. The reason is visible in the trajectories (Fig.~\ref{fig:puredriven}): on the sharp small-number wells the state is captured by whatever integer the partial count currently resembles and is dragged up through the wrong wells as the count grows, because a partial count is not diagnostic of the total. A partial count is thus a poor retrieval cue, unlike a degraded but complete estimate.

The width exponent $p$ is a free parameter of the model, shared across durations and fitted at $p = 1.86$ (Table~\ref{tab:params}). It grows the wells from narrow and well-separated at small $n$ to wide and overlapping at large $n$, but $p$ and the scale $\sigma_c$ are not interpreted singly. We therefore summarize their joint effect with the continuous width-based index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, defined by $\sigma_n=1$. What separates our account from the Weber/ANS magnitude code is the discrete pattern-completion architecture: at the identical trial-level convention the attractor beats the magnitude code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 838$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). Fitted sensory, lapse, and RT-link values for the joint winning model are reported in Table~\ref{tab:params} (\hyperref[sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}).

The comparison shares one landscape across durations. The attractor beats the Weber/ANS fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=838$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=855$) and the matched C\&P fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=11{,}453$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=11{,}355$).

\section{Supplementary Material 3: Parameter recovery}
\label{sm:recovery}

To test identifiability under the shared-landscape model, we generated $20$ complete synthetic datasets, each containing all five exposure durations. For each dataset, $\sigma_c$ and $p$ were jittered once and shared across durations, whereas $s_0$, $\lambda_0$, and lapse were jittered separately for each duration. Responses were sampled with the empirical trial count in every duration-by-numerosity cell, including the lapse channel, on a 1601-node quadrature grid. Each complete dataset was then refitted with the same categorical likelihood, 201-node fitting grid, and shared-landscape parameterization as the headline analysis. Thus, the landscape statistics contain $20$ independent fits and the duration-specific statistics contain $100$ fitted blocks. Table~\ref{tab:recovery} reports fitted and derived quantities; Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} shows their scatter and error correlations.

Sharing the landscape across all five durations makes its two fitted parameters identifiable at this sample size: identity $R^2$ is 0.980 for $\sigma_c$ and 0.997 for $p$. Their errors nevertheless trade off ($\rho=-0.87$), so the two coefficients should not be assigned separate psychological meanings. The derived width profile is recovered more directly: $R^2=0.994$ for $\sigma_3$ and $R^2=0.998$ for $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, whose median absolute error is $0.024$ numerosities. The two sensory parameters show the analogous trade-off ($\rho=-0.94$): within duration, $s_0$ alone is only moderately recovered ($R^2=0.538$), whereas the combined sensory-noise profile $\sigma(n)=\sqrt{s_{00}^2+s_0 n^{\lambda_0}}$ is recovered accurately ($R^2=0.991$ at $n=5$). Lapse recovery is also strong ($R^2=0.964$). These results support interpreting the width and sensory profiles rather than assigning causal meaning to their component coefficients; $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ remains a width-based summary, not an estimate of where attractors disappear.

\subsection*{Individual differences}

We ask whether a sharper landscape goes with lower estimation noise across participants using one hierarchical trial-level model with non-overlapping response channels (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}c). The analysis includes all $52{,}933$ responses from the $N=360$ sessions that completed the five exposure durations. Small-numerosity trials ($n\leq8$) enter the power-law attractor likelihood with participant effect $u_i^n$ on $\log n_{\mathrm{width}}$; the width exponent and duration-specific sensory channels are fixed at the all-duration group estimates. Larger-numerosity trials ($n\geq9$) enter a discretized linear Weber likelihood, $r^\star\sim\mathcal{N}(\gamma_d n,(w n)^2)$ on $r=1,\ldots,99$, with participant effect $u_i^w$ on $\log w$ and pooled duration-specific gain $\gamma_d$.

Participant lapse is estimated in the same model, $\operatorname{logit}(\ell_{i,d})=\alpha_{\ell,d}+u_i^\ell$, with a duration-specific intercept and a participant effect shared across durations and channels. The joint Gaussian distribution of $(u_i^n,u_i^w,u_i^\ell)$ is parameterized so that $\rho_{nw\cdot\ell}$ is the $n_{\mathrm{width}}$--Weber correlation conditional on lapse. Tensor Gauss--Hermite marginalization uses nine nodes per dimension (729 joint nodes). Two dispersed starts converge to the same alternative optimum; the $\rho_{nw\cdot\ell}=0$ likelihood-ratio null re-optimizes every nuisance parameter. Participant lapse covaries with both outcomes in the potentially confounding directions ($\hat\rho_{n\ell}=-.45$, $\hat\rho_{w\ell}=.59$), but the partial association remains $\hat\rho_{nw\cdot\ell}=-.627$ ($\chi^2(1)=105.0$, $p=1.2\times10^{-24}$). Main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}c plots duration-averaged empirical-Bayes estimates.
