\section{Supplementary Material 1: Trial-level fitting}
\label{sm:methods}

\paragraph{Data.} We reconstruct one row per trial from the openly available data of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, grouping by session (\texttt{uniqueid}) and trial and keeping the presented numerosity, the typed response, and the response time (converted to seconds). The source paper analysed four between-experiment arms at $N=100$ each after excluding the ten highest-error participants per arm; the open DURATION file pools the three duration-control arms (NONE / AREA / DENSITY) without that exclusion. Exposure duration is within participants: $379$ sessions contribute DURATION trials and $360$ completed all five exposures. We use the five exposure durations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640~ms), true numerosities $n = 1$--$15$, giving ${\approx}10{,}600$ trials per duration and $52{,}995$ trials in total. No trials are aggregated: the likelihood is evaluated over the individual integer reports. Empirically, the recorded response time runs from stimulus offset (mask onset) to typed-response submission: the gap between consecutive trial timestamps equals the next trial's exposure plus its recorded RT plus a residual that is constant across durations (median $1101$~ms), whereas measuring RT from stimulus onset would leave a residual that grows exactly with exposure. The exposure period is therefore excluded from the RT used here.

\paragraph{Sensory channel and well support.} The completed sensory estimate is $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(n,\,\sigma(n)^2\big)$ with $\sigma(n)=\sqrt{s_{00}^2 + s_0\,n^{\lambda_0}}$ and fixed baseline $s_{00}=0.05$; only $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ are fitted (per duration in the joint fit). Integer memory components sit at $m=1,\ldots,19$ (the true range $1$--$15$ plus four units of padding). The readout softmax and the energy $V$ are defined over this same support. Typed responses run up to $R_{\max}=99$; a few per cent fall outside the well support ($3.9$--$6.1\%$ across durations) and are handled by the lapse channel below.

\paragraph{Clean-up dynamics.} From $x=y_0$ the state follows the discrete descent $x\leftarrow x-\mathrm{clip}(\eta\,\partial V/\partial x,\,\pm\tfrac12\min_m\sigma_m)$ with fixed step size $\eta=1$ and global clamp $\tfrac12\min_m\sigma_m$ (equal to $\tfrac12\sigma_c$ for the power-law wells). Descent stops at the first iteration $t$ for which either $|\mathrm{step}|<\mathrm{tol}$ with $\mathrm{tol}=10^{-4}$, or the step reverses sign relative to the previous update (oscillation criterion: $\mathrm{step}_t\cdot\mathrm{step}_{t-1}<0$). In both cases the reversing or sub-threshold step is \emph{not} taken, so the resting state $x^*$ is the state at detection and the settling time is $T=t$. If neither criterion is met within the give-up budget $G$ iterations, the trajectory is truncated: $T=G$ and $x^*$ is the state after the $G$-th update. The RT links involving convergence time use this bounded step count $T$ (main-text Eq.~\ref{main-eq:rt}).

\paragraph{Give-up budget.} On the flat large-numerosity landscape the descent does not converge, so without a bound the settling time---and with it the predicted response time---would grow without limit. We therefore stop clean-up after $G$ steps. Profiling the joint response likelihood over $G\in\{40,60,100,120,140,160,250\}$, refitting the shared landscape $(\sigma_c,p)$ and the per-duration sensory channel and lapse at each value, places the optimum near $G\approx120$ ($\Delta\log L=0$); $G=100$ lies within about $5$ log-likelihood units of this optimum ($\Delta\log L=-5.3$), whereas $G\le60$ and $G\ge250$ are disfavoured by tens to hundreds of units ($\Delta\log L=-86$ and $-400$). Across the plausible window $G\in[100,160]$, the width index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ varies by less than $0.2$. We therefore use the profile-likelihood optimum, $G=120$, and count it as one shared free parameter of every clean-up model. Because the budget shapes the large-$n$ readout (mean estimate and exact-response rate), it is identified by the accuracy channel rather than by the response times, so using it to cap the settling time is not circular.

\paragraph{Response likelihood.} For each model and duration the response probability of Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:marginal}) marginalizes the readout over the sensory channel. The integral is computed \emph{deterministically}, so the objective is free of Monte-Carlo noise. Gauss--Hermite quadrature is inappropriate here: for the attractor models the clean-up map $y_0 \mapsto x^*$ is near-discontinuous (evidence snaps to basins), and Gauss--Hermite carries a $1.7$--$4\%$ error even at $241$ nodes. We therefore use a uniform standard-normal grid on $z\in[-7,7]$ with Gaussian weights for \emph{every} model, so the comparison is on identical footing. Against high-sample Monte-Carlo estimates, a grid of $801$ nodes matches the integral to $\le 2\times10^{-3}$ (mean $1.5\times10^{-4}$). The optimizer uses a cheaper grid ($201$ nodes); the reported log-likelihoods are recomputed at the fitted parameters on a fine grid ($1601$ nodes).

\paragraph{Lapse channel.} We never discard out-of-support responses: the final response probability is $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$ with $R_{\max} = 99$, a uniform contamination over $r=1,\ldots,99$ that places positive mass everywhere. The lapse is counted in every AIC and BIC. It is fitted separately at each duration in the attractor, static-readout, Weber/ANS, and C\&P fits. The published-form joint C\&P fit instead shares one lapse across durations; we therefore also fit a matched joint C\&P variant with duration-specific lapses.

\paragraph{Response-time channel.} Response time is modelled as log-normal, $\log \mathrm{rt} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(t_0 + m(n)), \sigma_{\mathrm{rt}})$, so main-text Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:rt}) is the location of this log-normal (the predicted geometric-mean RT), not an arithmetic-mean identity. The metric $m(n)$ is the expected posterior entropy ($\kappa\,\mathbb{E}[H]$), the expected convergence time ($\tau\,\mathbb{E}[T]$), or their sum (the hybrid). We fix the basal latency at $t_0=1.30$~s: because RT is timed from stimulus offset, this intercept absorbs mask-viewing, typing, and motor time rather than exposure duration, and at the longest exposures the geometric-mean RT for $n=1$ approaches ${\sim}1.30$~s, placing $t_0$ at the floor of the observed chronometry. It is held fixed for all models and durations and is not estimated from the likelihood. Link coefficients are shared across durations and residual scatter $\sigma_{\mathrm{rt}}$ is profiled per duration. The steps-only link is selected by AIC, narrowly ahead of the hybrid ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=0.24$) and far ahead of entropy alone ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=1579.5$). All three are fitted with the landscape held fixed at its response-fit values, providing a constrained cross-channel test of the fitted landscape.

\paragraph{Optimization.} The deterministic objective is minimized by \texttt{scipy} differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} followed by a Nelder--Mead polish, with fixed seeds.

\section{Supplementary Material 2: Model comparison and constrained fits}
\label{sm:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} consolidates the joint response-model fits and states the sharing configuration used for every row. In the power-law attractor and static fits, only the landscape parameters $(\sigma_c,p)$ are shared; in the constant-width ablations, $p=0$ is fixed and only $\sigma_c$ is shared. The sensory parameters $(s_0,\lambda_0)$ and lapse are duration-specific. The Weber/ANS model has no separable landscape, so its four parameters remain duration-specific.

The normative benchmark is the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, reimplemented from their published code: a Zipf ($1/n^2$) prior read out through an information-bottleneck posterior whose information budget is $\min(B, R\,t)$ bits, with $t$ the exposure duration. Its published joint form shares $B$, $R$, and one lapse across durations ($k=3$). Because the other joint models use duration-specific lapses, the matched C\&P comparison shares only $B$ and $R$ and fits five lapses ($k=7$); this is the C\&P row used in main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}a. Our implementation reproduces the published parameters on simulated data and matches the reference likelihood to machine precision.

Turning the clean-up dynamics on adds a single parameter, the shared give-up budget $G$ (\hyperref[sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}); the dynamics are otherwise deterministic given the landscape. Even net of this penalty, the attractor improves AIC by $721.6$ over its matched static readout in the joint fit. Fixing $p=0$, and hence $\sigma_n=\sigma_c$, worsens AIC by $3488.2$ (both models share the budget, so this comparison is unaffected). The matched constant-width static model performs worse still. Clean-up therefore remains beneficial under the ablation, but it cannot compensate for removing magnitude-dependent broadening.

A further alternative dispenses with the completed estimate altogether. In this purely driven model there is no sensory channel $\sigma(n)$: the items are registered one at a time over the exposure and the running count itself drives the attractor, while the landscape pulls the state toward the stored integers (main-text Methods), with the response read out at offset. It fits notably worse than the completed-estimate attractor (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The reason is visible in the trajectories (Fig.~\ref{fig:puredriven}): on the sharp small-number wells the state is captured by whatever integer the partial count currently resembles and is dragged up through the wrong wells as the count grows, because a partial count is not diagnostic of the total. A partial count is thus a poor retrieval cue, unlike a degraded but complete estimate. By contrast, supplying the same complete estimate gradually rather than at once, at matched reliability, leaves the fit essentially unchanged: the log-likelihood is flat over more than an order of magnitude in the coupling strength, so the attractor is indifferent to the timing of the evidence but not to its completeness.

The width exponent $p$ is a free parameter of the model, shared across durations and fitted at $p = 1.86$ (Table~\ref{tab:params}). It grows the wells from narrow and well-separated at small $n$ to wide and overlapping at large $n$, but $p$ and the scale $\sigma_c$ are not interpreted singly. We therefore summarize their joint effect with the continuous width-based index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, defined by $\sigma_n=1$ (\hyperref[sm:nwidth]{Supplementary Material~3}). What separates our account from the Weber/ANS magnitude code is not a particular width exponent or this proxy, but the discrete pattern-completion architecture: at the identical trial-level convention the attractor beats the magnitude code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 838$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). Fitted sensory, lapse, and RT-link values for the joint winning model are reported in Table~\ref{tab:params} (\hyperref[sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}).

The comparison shares one landscape across durations. The attractor beats the Weber/ANS fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=838$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=855$) and the matched C\&P fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=11{,}453$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=11{,}355$).

\section{Supplementary Material 3: Width-based resolution index}
\label{sm:nwidth}

The continuous width-based index is defined by $\sigma_n=1$, where the fitted Gaussian-component width equals the unit spacing between integers. Solving $\sigma_n=\sigma_c n^p=1$ gives $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$. This definition is equivalent to the heuristic $d'=1$~\citep{macmillan_detection_2004}. It summarizes the fitted width law with a single continuous resolution index.

The predicted proportion of exact responses shows a small shoulder near $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, even though the fitted width law and sensory-noise profile vary smoothly with $n$ (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:fit}b). The shoulder vanishes when clean-up is switched off at identical parameters (Fig.~\ref{fig:pexact}), so it reflects the dynamics on the integer lattice. Its numerical alignment with $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ does not make the width proxy a discrete exact-range boundary.

The shoulder occurs near this width-based proxy and disappears without clean-up.}

Last, we asked whether the fitted model also reproduces the effective Weber fraction. We computed the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the fitted response distribution, $(1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell/R_{\max}$, per numerosity and exposure (Fig.~\ref{fig:weber}). The CoV is elevated at the smallest numerosities, where the small denominator inflates $\mathrm{sd}/\mathrm{mean}$ in both model and data. It settles to a roughly constant plateau at larger numerosities. For continuity with the original analysis, we fit $w_{\mathrm{eff}}\propto n^\gamma$ over the pre-specified window $n\ge n_{\mathrm{width}}$. This gives a pooled exponent $\gamma=-0.20$ for the model and $-0.10$ for the data. Here $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ defines an analysis window, not the onset of an estimation regime.

\paragraph{Individual differences (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}c).} We also ask whether a sharper landscape goes with lower estimation noise across participants, using a bivariate hierarchical model that avoids overlapping summary statistics. Clustering is by session (\texttt{uniqueid}). We retain every typed response at 320 and 640~ms for the $366$ sessions that completed both exposures, and require at least one trial in each channel of a given split ($N=362$). Small-$n$ trials ($n\le n_{\mathrm{lo}}$) enter a power-law attractor categorical likelihood with participant random effect $u_i^{n}$ on $\log n_{\mathrm{width}}$ and duration-specific fixed effects; the width exponent $p$ and the sensory channel $(s_0,\lambda_0)$ are held at the group joint-winner values, and the duration-specific lapses use the main $R_{\max}=99$ convention. Large-$n$ trials ($n\ge n_{\mathrm{hi}}$) enter a discretized linear Weber model $r^\star\sim\mathcal{N}(\gamma_d\,n,(w\,n)^2)$ on $r=1,\ldots,99$, with participant random effect $u_i^{w}$ on $\log w$, duration-specific fixed effects, and a pooled duration-specific gain $\gamma_d$. The latent pair $(u_i^{n},u_i^{w})$ is bivariate normal with correlation $\rho$ (partial pooling via Gauss--Hermite marginalization). The primary split is $n_{\mathrm{lo}}=8$, $n_{\mathrm{hi}}=9$; neighbouring splits $7/8$ and $9/10$ are reported as robustness checks. At the primary split, $\hat\rho=-0.569$ (likelihood-ratio test against $\rho=0$: $\mathrm{LR}=156.9$, one-sided $p=2.8\times10^{-36}$); the neighbouring splits give $\hat\rho=-0.767$ and $-0.709$. Main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}c plots empirical-Bayes $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ against $w$ (duration-averaged) under the primary split.

\section{Supplementary Material 4: Exploratory reanalysis of developmental dyscalculia}
\label{sm:dyscalculia}

As an exploratory extension we applied the winning model to the exact-enumeration data of~\citet{decarli_impaired_2020}: accuracy and response time per subject and numerosity for $32$ children with developmental dyscalculia (DD) and $32$ age-matched controls, using numerosities $n = 1$--$7$ (dropping $n = 8$ exactly as the original authors do, for the guessing end-effect they report). The dot arrays were shown for a fixed exposure of $500$~ms, so all quantities in this section refer to that exposure. The model fits both groups well (Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}a,b) and again reproduces the widening well profile of the main dataset. We summarize each child's fitted width profile with the continuous index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, defined as in \hyperref[sm:nwidth]{Supplementary Material~3}.

First, the decline of accuracy with numerosity is better explained by changing the fitted well-width profile than by sensory noise. With an exhaustive per-subject grid search and a fixed common lapse and exponent, we fitted two single-mechanism variants to each child's accuracy curve. In the width-only variant, sensory noise is switched off and the decline is carried by broader, more overlapping wells. In the noise-only variant, the width profile is fixed and sensory noise carries the decline. The width-only variant fits better in $64/64$ subjects (mean SSE $0.010$ vs.\ $0.097$, Wilcoxon $p=3.5\times10^{-12}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}c). Within these two restricted single-mechanism variants, the width-only account fits the individual accuracy curves better than the noise-only account.

Second, the group difference is reflected in the fitted width profile. Children with DD have lower extrapolated $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ than age-matched controls (median $8.1$ vs.\ $8.8$, Mann--Whitney $p=0.018$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}}=-0.35$), whereas the mirror analysis that frees sensory noise instead shows only a weak trend ($p=0.05$) and fits worse. In rank terms, the effect is comparable to the raw accuracy deficit at $n=4$--$6$ ($r_{\mathrm{rb}}=-0.33$). The result is consistent with a coarser fitted numerical code in DD, although the analysis remains exploratory.

On this model, then, developmental dyscalculia is associated with a coarser fitted numerical code. It is also complementary to the account of~\citet{dolfi_weaker_2024}, who locate the dyscalculic deficit in a weaker number sense within an image-computable network. Both accounts place the deficit in the number code, and coupling the attractor to such a front end (which the present model sets aside) would allow a resolution deficit and a sensory-noise deficit to be separated within a single model. The analysis remains exploratory: the data are coarse ($7$ trials per cell), the localization to width profile rests on the per-subject model comparison rather than on a full identification of the two mechanisms, and a small sensory-noise component may coexist.
