\noindent This document accompanies the article \emph{An attractor account of numerosity perception}. Supplementary Material~1 details the trial-level fitting; Supplementary Material~2 reports the full model comparison and the constrained cross-duration fits; Supplementary Material~3 the parameter recovery; Supplementary Material~4 the resolution-limit prediction and its robustness; and Supplementary Material~5 the exploratory developmental-dyscalculia reanalysis.

\section{Supplementary Material 1: Trial-level fitting}
\label{sm:methods}

\paragraph{Data.} We reconstruct one row per trial from the openly available data of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, grouping by participant and trial and keeping the presented numerosity, the typed response, and the response time (converted to seconds). We use the five exposure durations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640~ms), true numerosities $n = 1$--$15$, giving ${\approx}10{,}600$ trials per duration and ${\approx}53{,}000$ in total. No trials are aggregated: the likelihood is evaluated over the individual integer reports.

\paragraph{Response likelihood.} For each model and duration the response probability of Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:marginal}) marginalises the readout over the sensory channel $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(n, \sigma(n))$. The integral is computed \emph{deterministically}, so the objective is free of Monte-Carlo noise. Gauss--Hermite quadrature is inappropriate here: for the attractor models the clean-up map $y_0 \mapsto x^*$ is near-discontinuous (evidence snaps to basins), and Gauss--Hermite carries a $1.7$--$4\%$ error even at $241$ nodes. We therefore use a uniform standard-normal grid with Gaussian weights for \emph{every} model, so the comparison is on identical footing. Against high-sample Monte-Carlo estimates, a grid of $801$ nodes matches the integral to $\le 2\times10^{-3}$ (mean $1.5\times10^{-4}$). The optimiser uses a cheaper grid ($201$ nodes); the reported log-likelihoods are recomputed at the fitted parameters on a fine grid ($1601$ nodes).

\paragraph{Lapse channel.} Typed responses run up to $99$, and a few per cent of guesses fall outside the integer-well support ($3.9$--$6.1\%$ across durations). We never discard them: the final response probability is $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$ with $R_{\max} = 99$, a uniform contamination that places positive mass everywhere. The lapse $\ell$ is fitted per duration under an identical convention across all models and is counted in AIC/BIC; it is stable across models (${\approx}0.06$--$0.08$).

\paragraph{Response-time and coupled channels.} Response time is modelled as log-normal, $\log \mathrm{rt} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(t_0 + m(n)), \sigma_{\mathrm{rt}})$, with the task-fixed base $t_0 = 1.30$~s. The metric $m(n)$ is the expected posterior entropy ($\kappa\,\mathbb{E}[H]$, available to every model), the expected convergence time ($\tau\,\mathbb{E}[T]$, attractor only), or their sum (the hybrid; Eq.~\ref{main-eq:rt}). Because the log-normal maximum-likelihood fit targets the mean of $\log \mathrm{rt}$, i.e.\ the geometric-mean RT, all reported $R^2$ values compare the prediction against the geometric mean rather than the median. The coupled $p(r, \mathrm{rt} \mid n)$ integrates response and RT over the same latent nodes. Co-optimising all channels together (warm-started from the two-stage fit) leaves the model ranking unchanged and barely moves the landscape, so we treat RT as a downstream cross-prediction.

\paragraph{Optimisation.} The deterministic objective is minimised by \texttt{scipy} differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} followed by a Nelder--Mead polish, with fixed seeds. The attractor models require a larger optimiser budget than the smooth static readouts (a low-budget fit understates them); re-fitting at a higher budget with a different seed changes the reported log-likelihoods by less than one part in $10^3$, so the fits are converged and the model ordering is not an optimisation artefact.

\section{Supplementary Material 2: Model comparison and constrained fits}
\label{sm:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} gives the full trial-level response comparison with each duration fitted separately; Table~\ref{tab:joint} gives the complementary joint fit, one landscape shared across all durations. Every model is fitted under the identical convention (per-duration parameters plus a per-duration lapse, summed over the five durations). The super-linear-width Gaussian attractor is the best account under both fits, and the ranking separates the contributions of its two ingredients: the clean-up dynamics and the width scaling.

Turning the clean-up dynamics on is a zero-parameter change (the dynamics are deterministic given the landscape), so the attractor and its static twin occupy the identical parameter space. Within the super-linear family this flips the fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 1256$ in favour of clean-up, and within the power-law family by $1326$, so the dynamics do explanatory work rather than add flexibility. (Per duration, clean-up helps strongly at 40--160~ms and is roughly neutral at 320--640~ms, where the attractor has largely settled during the exposure itself; main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}b.)

The width exponent is a free parameter of the model rather than a fixed value. It is estimated firmly super-linear at every exposure except the noise-dominated 40~ms flash (Table~\ref{tab:params}). This super-linearity is what gives the wells their sharp-to-broad profile: narrow and well-separated for small numbers, wide and overlapping for large ones. Granting the Weber/ANS code the same flexibility does not close the gap: freeing its width exponent (the power-law readout, also $k = 25$) leaves it $1491$ behind the winning attractor, and the freed exponent itself settles super-linear rather than at the Weber value $\sigma_n \propto n$. At equal parameter count, then, a magnitude code still loses, and it does not even prefer the Weber scaling.

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:ladder} fits each duration separately. Because the landscape is a property of the number system rather than of the display, we also fit a single landscape ($\sigma_c$ and the width exponent) shared across all five durations, leaving only the sensory channel and lapse free per duration (Table~\ref{tab:joint}; main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}a). The attractor remains the best account. With one shared landscape ($17$ parameters) it still beats the fully free per-duration Weber/ANS code ($20$ parameters) by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 905$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = 932$) and the normative proxy by $4136$, and the clean-up dynamics still improve the fit at equal parameters by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 781$. Forcing the landscape to be duration-independent costs $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 3529$ relative to the separate fits ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = 3458$), and the cost is concentrated in $\sigma_c$ (sharing only $\sigma_c$ costs $3398$, only $\beta$ costs $990$). The landscape therefore does change with exposure, mostly by sharpening: this is the resolution growth that drives $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (Supplementary Material~4). We read this as one mechanism whose resolution improves with viewing time, not a different mechanism per duration.

In the winning model the number frequency $f_n$ enters only through the well width. We also tested adding a frequency prior at the readout stage ($p(n \mid x^*) \propto f_n \exp g_n(x^*)$), a zero-parameter change. It does not robustly improve the fit (summed $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = -125$ but inconsistent in sign across durations, helping at 80--160~ms and hurting at 40, 320, and 640~ms), so we retain the flat readout and let the frequency dependence live entirely in the well width.

When the response and response-time channels are fitted together as a single coupled likelihood, rather than treating the response time as a downstream cross-prediction, the conclusions are unchanged: the winning attractor with the dynamics-based hybrid RT link remains best, ahead of the Weber/ANS code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 3913$. Within the winner, the convergence-time (hybrid) link improves this coupled fit over the static entropy proxy by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 784$, so the attractor dynamics carry the response-time channel even when it is fitted together with the response distribution.

\section{Supplementary Material 3: Parameter recovery}
\label{sm:recovery}

To confirm that the fitted quantities are identifiable at the trial level, we ran a parametric recovery: for each duration we generated $50$ synthetic datasets from parameters jittered around the fitted solution, sampled with the empirical per-numerosity trial counts and the lapse channel, and refitted them with the exact headline pipeline ($250$ refits in total). Table~\ref{tab:recovery} reports the recovery of each parameter and of the derived quantities the paper interprets; Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} shows the scatter and the error-correlation structure.

The width exponent $\beta$ and the derived resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$, the two quantities that carry the paper's argument, are recovered accurately ($R^2 = 0.89$ and $0.996$). The only strong trade-off in the error-correlation matrix is between the two sensory parameters $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ ($\rho = -0.97$); so we never interpret them separately, only through the combined sensory-noise profile $\sigma(n) = \sqrt{s_{00}^2 + s_0 n^{\lambda_0}}$, which is itself recovered accurately. All landscape quantities ($\sigma_c$, $\beta$, $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$) are cleanly identified.

\section{Supplementary Material 4: The resolution limit \texorpdfstring{$n_{\mathrm{crit}}$}{n\_crit}}
\label{sm:ncrit}

The critical numerosity is defined by adjacent-well discriminability $d' = 1$, i.e.\ $\sigma_n = 1$ (well standard deviation equal to unit spacing)~\citep{macmillan_detection_2004}. Solving $\sigma_n = (\sigma_c/f_n)^\beta = 1$ for the fitted parameters gives $n_{\mathrm{crit}} \approx 4.6,\,7.5,\,8.7,\,9.2,\,10.0$ for 40--640~ms. The crossing lies inside the fitted range at every duration and grows monotonically with exposure (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}e).

Because the crossing falls inside the fitted window, the estimate could in principle be shaped by the large-$n$ tail rather than by the small-number range itself. It is not: refitting on $n \le 10$ alone returns nearly the same width exponent and $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ as the full-range fit for 80--640~ms (e.g.\ $\beta = 1.88$ vs.\ $1.81$ at 80~ms, $2.09$ vs.\ $2.19$ at 320~ms; $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ within $0.3$ at all four), and only the noise-dominated 40~ms flash shifts (Fig.~\ref{fig:ncap}). Within $n \le 10$ the attractor still comes out ahead of its static twin ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 172$) and of the Weber/ANS code ($2043$). The exact code is thus a property of the small-number range, not an artefact of fitting the tail.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../output/trial_level_model_comparison/ncap10_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Small-range refit.} The fitted width exponent $\beta$ (\textbf{a}) and the resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (\textbf{b}), from the full-range fit ($n \le 15$, filled) and from a refit on the small range ($n \le 10$, open). Both are essentially unchanged for 80--640~ms; only the noise-dominated 40~ms flash shifts. The discrete, exact behaviour is intrinsic to the small-number range.}
  \label{fig:ncap}
\end{figure}

The response-time channel points to the same boundary. Within the exact range ($n < n_{\mathrm{crit}}$) the model tracks the geometric-mean response time closely; above it the monotone deliberation-time prediction keeps rising while the empirical RT plateaus, because participants have left the exact regime and are guessing approximately (Fig.~\ref{fig:rtdomain}). The onset of the empirical RT plateau thus coincides with $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$, even though the latter is derived from the accuracy channel alone. We therefore fit the latent-to-seconds RT link on all $n$ and report the within-domain agreement; truncating the fit to $n < n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is counter-productive, since at short durations the cut falls just before saturation and the truncated fit overshoots.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../output/trial_level_model_comparison/rt_domain.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Response time within and beyond the exact range.} Predicted geometric-mean RT (solid, $n < n_{\mathrm{crit}}$; dashed, extrapolated above) against the data. Within the exact range the model tracks RT; above $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ it over-predicts, the signature of the switch to approximate responding. The RT plateau onset coincides with the independently derived $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$.}
  \label{fig:rtdomain}

The resolution limit also leaves a visible mark in exact accuracy. The landscape is smooth by construction (the width law and the sensory-noise profile are smooth, monotone functions of $n$), yet the predicted proportion of exact responses shows a small shoulder at the resolution limit, visible in the main-text co-fit (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:fit}b). This is not extra fitted flexibility but the discrete clean-up basins on the fixed integer lattice: around $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ the last wells that still resolve snap noisy evidence back onto the exact answer and prop exact accuracy up at the edge of the exact range. The shoulder vanishes when the clean-up is switched off at identical parameters (Fig.~\ref{fig:pexact}). The shoulder thus tracks $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ across durations and is a signature of the mechanism rather than of additional degrees of freedom.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../output/trial_level_model_comparison/pexact_decomposition.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The exact-accuracy shoulder is a clean-up signature.} Predicted proportion of exact responses at 80~ms (\textbf{a}) and 640~ms (\textbf{b}) with the clean-up dynamics on (solid) and off (dashed), at identical parameters; dotted verticals mark $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$. The shoulder sits at $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ and disappears without clean-up: it reflects the discrete basins on the integer lattice, not extra parameters.}
  \label{fig:pexact}
\end{figure}

Last, we asked whether the fitted model also reproduces the effective Weber fraction. We computed the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the fitted response distribution, $(1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell/R_{\max}$, per numerosity and exposure (Fig.~\ref{fig:weber}). The CoV is elevated at the smallest numerosities (where the ratio $\mathrm{sd}/\mathrm{mean}$ is inflated by the small denominator, in model and data alike) and settles to a roughly constant plateau across the estimation range ($n \ge n_{\mathrm{crit}}$). A power-law fit $w_{\mathrm{eff}} \propto n^{\gamma}$ over the estimation range gives a pooled exponent $\gamma = -0.21$ for the model and $-0.17$ for the data: approximately scalar variability, close to the Weber value $\gamma = 0$. The same landscape thus reproduces the numerosity variability, and its slope, across the whole range.

\section{Supplementary Material 5: Exploratory reanalysis of developmental dyscalculia}
\label{sm:dyscalculia}

As an exploratory extension we applied the winning model to the exact-enumeration data of~\citet{decarli_impaired_2020}: accuracy and response time per subject and numerosity for $32$ children with developmental dyscalculia (DD) and $32$ age-matched controls, using numerosities $n = 1$--$7$ (dropping $n = 8$ exactly as the original authors do, for the guessing end-effect they report). The dot arrays were shown for a fixed exposure of $500$~ms, so all quantities in this section, including $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$, refer to that exposure. The model fits both groups well (Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}a,b) and again prefers a super-linear width ($\beta \approx 1.5$--$1.6$), replicating the main dataset. The fitted $n_{\mathrm{crit}}(\sigma_n = 1) \approx 8$ falls slightly below the adult values of $9.2$--$10.0$ at $320$--$640$~ms (Supplementary Material~4), as one would expect for children. Although these fits on 64 subjects can only give us very preliminary intuitions, we can make two tentative observations.

First, the decline of accuracy with numerosity is better explained by a loss of resolution than by sensory noise. With an exhaustive per-subject grid search and a fixed common lapse, we fitted two single-mechanism variants of the model to each child's accuracy curve: a resolution-only variant, in which sensory noise is switched off and the decline is carried entirely by the merging of wells, and a noise-only variant, in which the wells never merge and the decline is carried entirely by sensory noise. The resolution-only variant fits better in $64/64$ subjects (mean SSE $0.010$ vs.\ $0.079$, Wilcoxon $p = 3.5\times10^{-12}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}c). The decline of exact enumeration in this dataset therefore behaves as a loss of discrete resolution, which supports the attractor framework on independent data and constrains where a group difference can arise.

Second, the group difference falls on that same resolution parameter. Per subject, the resolution limit is shorter in DD ($n_{\mathrm{crit}} = 8.2$ vs.\ $9.1$ for controls; Mann--Whitney $p = 0.007$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.39$ with the RT channel included; $p = 0.044$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.29$ in the accuracy-only fits of Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}d), whereas the mirror analysis that frees sensory noise instead shows only a weak trend ($p = 0.07$) and fits worse. In rank terms the $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ difference is comparable to the raw accuracy deficit at $n = 4$--$6$ ($r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.33$): the shift of ${\approx}0.9$ numerosities looks small only because of the scale it is expressed on.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../output/decarli_fit/decarli_dd_summary.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Developmental dyscalculia as a shorter exact number code.} (a)~Exact accuracy per numerosity (points, group mean $\pm$ SEM; lines, the group-level model fit). (b)~Median response time, same conventions. (c)~Per-subject accuracy SSE of the two single-mechanism fits: the resolution-only model fits better than the noise-only model in $64/64$ subjects (all points below the diagonal). (d)~Per-subject resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ ($\sigma_n = 1$) from the resolution-only accuracy fits, at the task's $500$~ms exposure. Boxes, median and interquartile range; whiskers, $1.5\times$ IQR; points, individual subjects; the bracket gives the Mann--Whitney $p$ and the rank-biserial correlation $r_{\mathrm{rb}}$ (negative: DD resolve to fewer numerosities) for this accuracy-only analysis.}
  \label{fig:decarli}
\end{figure}

On this model, then, developmental dyscalculia is not a noisier perception of number but a coarser exact code: the discrete resolution runs out earlier, at the subitizing edge. The claim is mechanistic and falsifiable, although much of the statistical power comes from analysing subjects individually rather than from the model itself. It is also complementary to the account of~\citet{dolfi_weaker_2024}, who locate the dyscalculic deficit in a weaker number sense within an image-computable network. Both accounts place the deficit in the number code, and coupling the attractor to such a front end (which the present model sets aside) would allow a resolution deficit and a sensory-noise deficit to be separated within a single model. The analysis remains exploratory: the data are coarse ($8$ trials per cell), the localisation to resolution rests on the per-subject model comparison rather than on a full identification of the two mechanisms, and a small sensory-noise component may coexist.
