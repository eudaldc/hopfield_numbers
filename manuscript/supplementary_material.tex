\noindent This document accompanies the article \emph{An attractor account of numerosity perception}. Supplementary Material~1 details the trial-level fitting; Supplementary Material~2 reports the full model comparison and the constrained cross-duration fits; Supplementary Material~3 the parameter recovery; Supplementary Material~4 the resolution-limit prediction and its robustness; and Supplementary Material~5 the exploratory developmental-dyscalculia reanalysis.

\section{Supplementary Material 1: Trial-level fitting}
\label{sm:methods}

\paragraph{Data.} We reconstruct one row per trial from the openly available data of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, grouping by participant and trial and keeping the presented numerosity, the typed response, and the response time (converted to seconds). We use the five exposure durations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640~ms), true numerosities $n = 1$--$15$, giving ${\approx}10{,}600$ trials per duration and ${\approx}53{,}000$ in total. No trials are aggregated: the likelihood is evaluated over the individual integer reports.

\paragraph{Response likelihood.} For each model and duration the response probability of Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:marginal}) marginalises the readout over the sensory channel $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(n, \sigma(n))$. The integral is computed \emph{deterministically}, so the objective is free of Monte-Carlo noise. Gauss--Hermite quadrature is inappropriate here: for the attractor models the clean-up map $y_0 \mapsto x^*$ is near-discontinuous (evidence snaps to basins), and Gauss--Hermite carries a $1.7$--$4\%$ error even at $241$ nodes. We therefore use a uniform standard-normal grid with Gaussian weights for \emph{every} model, so the comparison is on identical footing. Against high-sample Monte-Carlo estimates, a grid of $801$ nodes matches the integral to $\le 2\times10^{-3}$ (mean $1.5\times10^{-4}$). The optimiser uses a cheaper grid ($201$ nodes); the reported log-likelihoods are recomputed at the fitted parameters on a fine grid ($1601$ nodes).

\paragraph{Lapse channel.} Typed responses run up to $99$, and a few per cent of guesses fall outside the integer-well support ($3.9$--$6.1\%$ across durations). We never discard them: the final response probability is $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$ with $R_{\max} = 99$, a uniform contamination that places positive mass everywhere. The lapse $\ell$ is fitted per duration under an identical convention across all models and is counted in AIC/BIC; it is stable across models (${\approx}0.06$--$0.08$).

\paragraph{Response-time channel.} Response time is modelled as log-normal, $\log \mathrm{rt} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(t_0 + m(n)), \sigma_{\mathrm{rt}})$, with the task-fixed base $t_0 = 1.30$~s. The metric $m(n)$ is the expected posterior entropy ($\kappa\,\mathbb{E}[H]$, available to every model), the expected convergence time ($\tau\,\mathbb{E}[T]$, attractor only), or their sum (the hybrid; Eq.~\ref{main-eq:rt}). The RT parameters are fitted to the response times with the landscape held fixed at its response-fit values, so the response-time channel is a cross-prediction of the fitted landscape.

\paragraph{Optimisation.} The deterministic objective is minimised by \texttt{scipy} differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} followed by a Nelder--Mead polish, with fixed seeds. The attractor models require a larger optimiser budget than the smooth static readouts (a low-budget fit understates them); re-fitting at a higher budget with a different seed changes the reported log-likelihoods by less than one part in $10^3$, so the fits are converged and the model ordering is not an optimisation artefact.

\section{Supplementary Material 2: Model comparison and constrained fits}
\label{sm:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} gives the full trial-level response comparison with each duration fitted separately; Table~\ref{tab:joint} gives the complementary joint fit, one landscape shared across all durations. Every model is fitted under the identical convention (per-duration parameters plus a per-duration lapse, summed over the five durations). The power-law-width Gaussian attractor is the best account under both fits, and the ranking separates the contributions of its two ingredients: the clean-up dynamics and the width scaling.

The normative benchmark is the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, reimplemented from their published code: a Zipf ($1/n^2$) prior read out through an information-bottleneck posterior whose per-duration information budget is $\min(B, R\,t)$ bits, with $t$ the exposure duration. We refit it under the same trial-level response likelihood as every other model. For the joint fit (Table~\ref{tab:joint}) it uses its published form, sharing its three parameters ($B$, $R$, and a lapse) across all durations; for the separate fit (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}) we grant it its most generous per-duration version, freeing the information budget at each duration. The model reproduces its published parameters when refit to their own simulated data ($B \approx 4.8$, $R \approx 41$), and our implementation matches their likelihood to machine precision.

Turning the clean-up dynamics on is a zero-parameter change (the dynamics are deterministic given the landscape), so the attractor and its static twin occupy the identical parameter space. This flips the fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 1327$ in favour of clean-up with separate fits, and by $641$ under the shared landscape, so the dynamics do explanatory work without added flexibility. (Per duration, clean-up helps strongly at 40--160~ms and is roughly neutral at 320--640~ms, where the attractor has largely settled during the exposure itself; main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}b.)

The width exponent $p$ is a free parameter of the model (Table~\ref{tab:params}). Its point estimates grow the wells from narrow and well-separated at small $n$ to wide and overlapping at large $n$, but the exponent and the scale $\sigma_c$ trade off strongly and are not identifiable singly (Supplementary Material~3), so we do not interpret the exponent on its own; the identifiable, behaviourally meaningful summary of the width law is the resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$. What separates our account from the Weber/ANS magnitude code is therefore not a particular width exponent but the discrete pattern-completion architecture: at the identical trial-level convention the attractor beats the magnitude code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 4270$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}).

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:ladder} fits each duration separately. Because the landscape is a property of the number system rather than of the display, we also fit a single landscape ($\sigma_c$ and the width exponent $p$) shared across all five durations, leaving only the sensory channel and lapse free per duration (Table~\ref{tab:joint}; main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}a). The attractor remains the best account. With one shared landscape ($17$ parameters) it still beats the fully free per-duration Weber/ANS code ($20$ parameters) by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 757$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = 783$) and the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020} ($3$ parameters) by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 12{,}026$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = 11{,}901$), and the clean-up dynamics still improve the fit at equal parameters by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 641$. Forcing the landscape to be duration-independent costs $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 3513$ relative to the separate fits ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = 3482$). The landscape therefore does change with exposure, mostly by sharpening: this is the resolution growth that drives $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (Supplementary Material~4). We read this as one mechanism whose resolution improves with viewing time, not a different mechanism per duration.

\section{Supplementary Material 3: Parameter recovery}
\label{sm:recovery}

To confirm that the fitted quantities are identifiable at the trial level, we ran a parametric recovery: for each duration we generated $20$ synthetic datasets from parameters jittered around the fitted solution, sampled with the empirical per-numerosity trial counts and the lapse channel, and refitted them with the exact headline pipeline ($100$ refits in total). Table~\ref{tab:recovery} reports the recovery of each parameter and of the derived quantities the paper interprets; Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} shows the scatter and the error-correlation structure.

  \centering

Scale parameters are compared on the log scale. The interpreted quantities are the derived ones (lower block): $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is a heavy-tailed transform of the strongly traded-off $(\sigma_c, p)$, so its identity $R^2$ is uninformative and it is summarised by rank.}
  \label{tab:recovery}
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
  \hline

  \hline

  $s_0$ (sensory scale) & 0.82 & 0.94 \\
  $\lambda_0$ (sensory exponent) & 0.41 & 0.83 \\

The individual landscape parameters, the scale $\sigma_c$ and the exponent $p$, trade off strongly (error correlation $\rho = -0.97$) and are not identifiable on their own, as do the two sensory parameters $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ ($\rho = -0.98$): the data pin down where the exact code breaks, not the scale and slope independently. We therefore never interpret these parameters singly, only through the quantities the argument actually uses, which recover well: the combined sensory-noise profile $\sigma(n) = \sqrt{s_{00}^2 + s_0 n^{\lambda_0}}$ ($\sigma(n=5)$, $R^2 = 0.99$), the well width in the exact range ($\sigma_3$, $R^2 = 0.89$), the lapse ($R^2 = 0.96$), and the resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$, which recovers in rank (Spearman $\rho = 0.98$, median error $0.04$ numerosities). Since $n_{\mathrm{crit}} = \sigma_c^{-1/p}$ is a heavy-tailed transform of the traded-off pair, a handful of ridge fits admit a low-exponent solution of near-equal likelihood that inflates the pointwise estimate; the quantity is robust as the ordinal measure the paper uses (it grows with exposure, and is shorter in dyscalculia).

\section{Supplementary Material 4: The resolution limit \texorpdfstring{$n_{\mathrm{crit}}$}{n\_crit}}
\label{sm:ncrit}

The critical numerosity is defined by adjacent-well discriminability $d' = 1$, i.e.\ $\sigma_n = 1$ (well standard deviation equal to unit spacing)~\citep{macmillan_detection_2004}. Solving $\sigma_n = \sigma_c\,n^{p} = 1$ for the fitted parameters, i.e.\ $n_{\mathrm{crit}} = \sigma_c^{-1/p}$, gives $n_{\mathrm{crit}} \approx 3.5,\,6.7,\,8.3,\,9.1,\,9.8$ for 40--640~ms. The crossing lies inside the fitted range at every duration and grows monotonically with exposure (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}e).

Because the crossing falls inside the fitted window, the estimate could in principle be shaped by the large-$n$ tail rather than by the small-number range itself. It is not: refitting on $n \le 10$ alone returns nearly the same width exponent and $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ as the full-range fit at every duration (e.g.\ $p = 1.67$ vs.\ $1.50$ at 80~ms, $1.78$ vs.\ $1.99$ at 320~ms; $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ within $0.4$ throughout; Fig.~\ref{fig:ncap}). Within $n \le 10$ the attractor still comes out ahead of its static twin ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 271$) and of the Weber/ANS code ($1900$). The exact code is thus a property of the small-number range, not an artefact of fitting the tail.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../output/trial_level_model_comparison/ncap10_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Small-range refit.} The fitted width exponent $p$ (\textbf{a}) and the resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ (\textbf{b}), from the full-range fit ($n \le 15$, filled) and from a refit on the small range ($n \le 10$, open). Both are essentially unchanged at every duration. The discrete, exact behaviour is intrinsic to the small-number range.}
  \label{fig:ncap}
\end{figure}

The response-time channel points to the same boundary. Within the exact range ($n < n_{\mathrm{crit}}$) the model tracks the geometric-mean response time closely; above it the monotone deliberation-time prediction keeps rising while the empirical RT plateaus, because participants have left the exact regime and are guessing approximately (Fig.~\ref{fig:rtdomain}). The onset of the empirical RT plateau thus coincides with $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$, even though the latter is derived from the accuracy channel alone. We therefore fit the latent-to-seconds RT link on all $n$ and report the within-domain agreement; truncating the fit to $n < n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is counter-productive, since at short durations the cut falls just before saturation and the truncated fit overshoots.

Within the exact range the model tracks RT; above $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ it over-predicts, the signature of the switch to approximate responding. The RT plateau onset coincides with the independently derived $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$.}

The resolution limit also leaves a visible mark in exact accuracy. The landscape is smooth by construction (the width law and the sensory-noise profile are smooth, monotone functions of $n$), yet the predicted proportion of exact responses shows a small shoulder at the resolution limit, visible in the main-text fit (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:fit}b). This is not extra fitted flexibility but the discrete clean-up basins on the fixed integer lattice: around $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ the last wells that still resolve snap noisy evidence back onto the exact answer and prop exact accuracy up at the edge of the exact range. The shoulder vanishes when the clean-up is switched off at identical parameters (Fig.~\ref{fig:pexact}). The shoulder thus tracks $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ across durations and is a signature of the mechanism.

  \caption{\textbf{The exact-accuracy shoulder is a clean-up signature.} Predicted proportion of exact responses at 80~ms (\textbf{a}) and 640~ms (\textbf{b}) with the clean-up dynamics on (solid) and off (dashed), at identical parameters; dotted verticals mark $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$. The shoulder sits at $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ and disappears without clean-up: it reflects the discrete basins on the integer lattice, not extra parameters.}
  \label{fig:pexact}
\end{figure}

Last, we asked whether the fitted model also reproduces the effective Weber fraction. We computed the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the fitted response distribution, $(1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell/R_{\max}$, per numerosity and exposure (Fig.~\ref{fig:weber}). The CoV is elevated at the smallest numerosities (where the ratio $\mathrm{sd}/\mathrm{mean}$ is inflated by the small denominator, in model and data alike) and settles to a roughly constant plateau across the estimation range ($n \ge n_{\mathrm{crit}}$). A power-law fit $w_{\mathrm{eff}} \propto n^{\gamma}$ over the estimation range gives a pooled exponent $\gamma = -0.18$ for the model, matching the $-0.16$ measured in the data: the model reproduces the empirical effective Weber fraction and its mild slope. The same landscape thus reproduces the numerosity variability, and its slope, across the whole range.

\section{Supplementary Material 5: Exploratory reanalysis of developmental dyscalculia}
\label{sm:dyscalculia}

As an exploratory extension we applied the winning model to the exact-enumeration data of~\citet{decarli_impaired_2020}: accuracy and response time per subject and numerosity for $32$ children with developmental dyscalculia (DD) and $32$ age-matched controls, using numerosities $n = 1$--$7$ (dropping $n = 8$ exactly as the original authors do, for the guessing end-effect they report). The dot arrays were shown for a fixed exposure of $500$~ms, so all quantities in this section, including $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$, refer to that exposure. The model fits both groups well (Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}a,b) and again reproduces the widening well profile of the main dataset. The fitted $n_{\mathrm{crit}}(\sigma_n = 1) \approx 8$ falls slightly below the adult values of $9.1$--$9.8$ at $320$--$640$~ms (Supplementary Material~4), as one would expect for children. Although these fits on 64 subjects can only give us very preliminary intuitions, we can make two tentative observations.

First, the decline of accuracy with numerosity is better explained by a loss of resolution than by sensory noise. With an exhaustive per-subject grid search and a fixed common lapse and exponent, we fitted two single-mechanism variants of the model to each child's accuracy curve: a resolution-only variant, in which sensory noise is switched off and the decline is carried entirely by the merging of wells, and a noise-only variant, in which the wells never merge and the decline is carried entirely by sensory noise. The resolution-only variant fits better in $64/64$ subjects (mean SSE $0.010$ vs.\ $0.097$, Wilcoxon $p = 3.5\times10^{-12}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}c). The decline of exact enumeration in this dataset therefore behaves as a loss of discrete resolution, which supports the attractor framework on independent data and constrains where a group difference can arise.

Second, the group difference falls on that same resolution parameter. Per subject, the resolution limit is shorter in DD ($n_{\mathrm{crit}} = 8.1$ vs.\ $8.8$ for controls; Mann--Whitney $p = 0.018$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.35$ with the RT channel included; $p = 0.044$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.29$ in the accuracy-only fits of Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}d), whereas the mirror analysis that frees sensory noise instead shows only a weak trend ($p = 0.05$) and fits worse. In rank terms the $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ difference is comparable to the raw accuracy deficit at $n = 4$--$6$ ($r_{\mathrm{rb}} = -0.33$): the shift of ${\approx}0.8$ numerosities looks small only because of the scale it is expressed on.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../output/decarli_fit/decarli_dd_summary.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Developmental dyscalculia as a shorter exact number code.} (a)~Exact accuracy per numerosity (points, group mean $\pm$ SEM; lines, the group-level model fit). (b)~Median response time, same conventions. (c)~Per-subject accuracy SSE of the two single-mechanism fits: the resolution-only model fits better than the noise-only model in $64/64$ subjects (all points below the diagonal). (d)~Per-subject resolution limit $n_{\mathrm{crit}}$ ($\sigma_n = 1$) from the resolution-only accuracy fits, at the task's $500$~ms exposure.   \label{fig:decarli}

On this model, then, developmental dyscalculia is modelled as a coarser exact code: the discrete resolution runs out earlier, at the subitizing edge. It is also complementary to the account of~\citet{dolfi_weaker_2024}, who locate the dyscalculic deficit in a weaker number sense within an image-computable network. Both accounts place the deficit in the number code, and coupling the attractor to such a front end (which the present model sets aside) would allow a resolution deficit and a sensory-noise deficit to be separated within a single model. The analysis remains exploratory: the data are coarse ($7$ trials per cell), the localisation to resolution rests on the per-subject model comparison rather than on a full identification of the two mechanisms, and a small sensory-noise component may coexist.
