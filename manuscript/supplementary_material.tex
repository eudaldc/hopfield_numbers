\noindent This document accompanies the article \emph{An attractor account of numerosity perception}. Supplementary Material~1 details the trial-level fitting; Supplementary Material~2 reports the full model comparison and the constrained cross-duration fits; Supplementary Material~3 the parameter recovery; Supplementary Material~4 the resolution-limit prediction and its robustness; and Supplementary Material~5 the exploratory developmental-dyscalculia reanalysis.

\section{Supplementary Material 1: Trial-level fitting}
\label{sm:methods}

\paragraph{Data.} We reconstruct one row per trial from the openly available data of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, grouping by participant and trial and keeping the presented numerosity, the typed response, and the response time (converted to seconds). We use the five exposure durations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640~ms), true numerosities $n = 1$--$15$, giving ${\approx}10{,}600$ trials per duration and ${\approx}53{,}000$ in total. No trials are aggregated: the likelihood is evaluated over the individual integer reports.

\paragraph{Response likelihood.} For each model and duration the response probability of Eq.~(\ref{main-eq:marginal}) marginalises the readout over the sensory channel $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(n, \sigma(n))$. The integral is computed \emph{deterministically}, so the objective is free of Monte-Carlo noise. Gauss--Hermite quadrature is inappropriate here: for the attractor models the clean-up map $y_0 \mapsto x^*$ is near-discontinuous (evidence snaps to basins), and Gauss--Hermite carries a $1.7$--$4\%$ error even at $241$ nodes. We therefore use a uniform standard-normal grid with Gaussian weights for \emph{every} model, so the comparison is on identical footing. Against high-sample Monte-Carlo estimates, a grid of $801$ nodes matches the integral to $\le 2\times10^{-3}$ (mean $1.5\times10^{-4}$). The optimiser uses a cheaper grid ($201$ nodes); the reported log-likelihoods are recomputed at the fitted parameters on a fine grid ($1601$ nodes).

\paragraph{Lapse channel.} Typed responses run up to $99$, and a few per cent of guesses fall outside the integer-well support ($3.9$--$6.1\%$ across durations). We never discard them: the final response probability is $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$ with $R_{\max} = 99$, a uniform contamination that places positive mass everywhere. The lapse is counted in every AIC and BIC. It is fitted separately at each duration in the attractor, static-readout, Weber/ANS, and separate C\&P fits. The published-form joint C\&P fit instead shares one lapse across durations; we therefore also fit a matched joint C\&P variant with duration-specific lapses.

\paragraph{Response-time channel.} Response time is modelled as log-normal, $\log \mathrm{rt} \sim \mathcal{N}(\log(t_0 + m(n)), \sigma_{\mathrm{rt}})$, with the task-fixed base $t_0 = 1.30$~s. The metric $m(n)$ is the expected posterior entropy ($\kappa\,\mathbb{E}[H]$, available to every model), the expected convergence time ($\tau\,\mathbb{E}[T]$, attractor only), or their sum (the hybrid; Eq.~\ref{main-eq:rt}). The RT parameters are fitted to response times with the landscape held fixed at its response-fit values, providing a constrained cross-channel test of the fitted landscape.

\paragraph{Optimisation.} The deterministic objective is minimised by \texttt{scipy} differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} followed by a Nelder--Mead polish, with fixed seeds. The attractor models require a larger optimiser budget than the smooth static readouts (a low-budget fit understates them); re-fitting at a higher budget with a different seed changes the reported log-likelihoods by less than one part in $10^3$, so the fits are converged and the model ordering is not an optimisation artefact.

\section{Supplementary Material 2: Model comparison and constrained fits}
\label{sm:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} consolidates the joint and separate response-model fits and states the sharing configuration used for every row. In the joint power-law attractor and static fits, only the landscape parameters $(\sigma_c,p)$ are shared; in the constant-width ablations, $p=0$ is fixed and only $\sigma_c$ is shared. The sensory parameters $(s_0,\lambda_0)$ and lapse are duration-specific. The Weber/ANS model has no separable landscape, so its four parameters remain duration-specific in both comparisons. The separate analysis fits all free parameters independently at each duration.

The normative benchmark is the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, reimplemented from their published code: a Zipf ($1/n^2$) prior read out through an information-bottleneck posterior whose information budget is $\min(B, R\,t)$ bits, with $t$ the exposure duration. Its published joint form shares $B$, $R$, and one lapse across durations ($k=3$). Because the other joint models use duration-specific lapses, the matched C\&P comparison shares only $B$ and $R$ and fits five lapses ($k=7$); this is the C\&P row used in main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}a. The separate C\&P analysis frees both the information budget and lapse at each duration ($k=10$), removing the $\min(B,Rt)$ rate-law constraint. Our implementation reproduces the published parameters on simulated data and matches the reference likelihood to machine precision.

Turning the clean-up dynamics on is a zero-parameter change because the dynamics are deterministic given the landscape. The attractor improves AIC by $1326.7$ in the separate analysis and by $640.6$ in the joint analysis. Per duration, clean-up helps strongly at 40--160~ms and is roughly neutral at 320--640~ms (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}b). Fixing $p=0$, and hence $\sigma_n=\sigma_c$, worsens AIC by $6636.4$ in the separate analysis and by $3405.3$ in the joint analysis. The matched constant-width static model performs worse still. Clean-up therefore remains beneficial under the ablation, but it cannot compensate for removing magnitude-dependent broadening.

The width exponent $p$ is a free parameter of the model (Table~\ref{tab:params}). Its point estimates grow the wells from narrow and well-separated at small $n$ to wide and overlapping at large $n$, but the exponent and the scale $\sigma_c$ trade off strongly and are not identifiable singly. We therefore summarize their joint effect with the continuous width-based index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, defined by $\sigma_n=1$ (Supplementary Material~3). What separates our account from the Weber/ANS magnitude code is not a particular width exponent or this proxy, but the discrete pattern-completion architecture: at the identical trial-level convention the attractor beats the magnitude code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 4270$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}).

The primary comparison shares one landscape across durations. The attractor still beats the fully duration-specific Weber/ANS fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=756.6$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=783.3$). It beats the matched C\&P fit by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=11{,}372.0$ ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=11{,}283.3$). Allowing C\&P a separate lapse at each duration improves its AIC by $653.7$ relative to its original shared-lapse form, but does not alter the model ordering. Forcing the attractor landscape to be duration-independent costs $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=3513.2$ relative to the separate fits ($\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=3482.4$). The duration-specific fits therefore reveal an exposure-dependent gain in effective resolution, represented here by narrower fitted wells. A driven-attractor formulation would be required to derive this temporal change within a trial.

\section{Supplementary Material 3: Parameter recovery}
\label{sm:recovery}

To confirm that the fitted quantities are identifiable at the trial level, we ran a parametric recovery: for each duration we generated $20$ synthetic datasets from parameters jittered around the fitted solution, sampled with the empirical per-numerosity trial counts and the lapse channel, and refitted them with the exact headline pipeline ($100$ refits in total). Table~\ref{tab:recovery} reports the recovery of each parameter and of the derived quantities the paper interprets; Figure~\ref{fig:recovery} shows the scatter and the error-correlation structure.

The landscape scale $\sigma_c$ and exponent $p$ trade off strongly (error correlation $\rho=-0.97$) and are not identifiable on their own, as do the two sensory parameters $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ ($\rho=-0.98$). The data constrain the combined width profile rather than its scale and slope independently. We therefore interpret the combined sensory-noise profile $\sigma(n)=\sqrt{s_{00}^2+s_0 n^{\lambda_0}}$ ($\sigma(n=5)$, $R^2=0.99$), the fitted width $\sigma_3$ ($R^2=0.89$), the lapse ($R^2=0.96$), and the continuous index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, which recovers in rank (Spearman $\rho=0.98$, median error $0.04$ numerosities). Since $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$ is a heavy-tailed transform of the traded-off pair, a handful of ridge fits admit a low-exponent solution of near-equal likelihood that inflates the pointwise estimate. The index is robust for ordinal comparisons of the fitted width profile, but it is not an estimate of where attractors disappear.

\section{Supplementary Material 4: Width-based and topological resolution limits}
\label{sm:nwidth}

We report two resolution indices with different meanings. The continuous width-based index is defined by $\sigma_n=1$, where the fitted Gaussian-component width equals the unit spacing between integers. Solving $\sigma_n=\sigma_c n^p=1$ gives $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$. This definition is equivalent to the heuristic $d'=1$~\citep{macmillan_detection_2004}. It summarizes the fitted width law and does not test whether an integer remains a local attractor.

The topological index is computed from the fitted total potential, $V(x)=-\log\sum_n\exp(g_n(x))$, over all 19 wells used by the response model. For each fit, we evaluated $\partial V/\partial x$ on a dense grid over $x\in[1,19]$, bracketed every sign change, and refined each root with Brent's method. The sign of $\partial^2V/\partial x^2$ classified each stationary point as a local minimum or maximum. We assigned each minimum injectively to its nearest integer. For adjacent assigned minima, the barrier height was $V(x_{\max})-\max\{V(x_{\min,L}),V(x_{\min,R})\}$, where $x_{\max}$ is the intervening maximum. We then defined $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$ as the largest consecutive integer from one that retained its own minimum and a positive barrier from the preceding basin.

The two indices differed in every fit (Table~\ref{tab:topological-limit}; main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:comparison}e). The duration-specific $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ values increased monotonically from 3.48 to 9.77, whereas $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$ increased from 2 to 7 and then remained at 7 between 320 and 640~ms. The joint landscape gave $n_{\mathrm{width}}=8.32$ and $n_{\mathrm{topo}}=6$. Their numerical difference is expected because $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ summarizes component width, whereas $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$ tests basin structure directly.

We repeated the complete stationary-point analysis with 5,000, 20,000, and 80,000 grid points before root refinement. All six fits retained identical stationary-point classifications, integer assignments, and $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$ values at every resolution. The largest displacement of any refined root was $2.4\times10^{-14}$ units of the number line. The reported topological limits are therefore insensitive to grid resolution.

Because $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ falls inside the fitted window, it could in principle be shaped by the large-$n$ tail rather than by the small-number range itself. It is not: refitting on $n \le 10$ alone returns nearly the same width exponent and $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ as the full-range fit at every duration (e.g.\ $p = 1.67$ vs.\ $1.50$ at 80~ms, $1.78$ vs.\ $1.99$ at 320~ms; $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ within $0.4$ throughout; Fig.~\ref{fig:ncap}). Within $n \le 10$ the attractor still comes out ahead of its static twin ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC} = 271$) and of the Weber/ANS code ($1900$). This refit establishes that the width-based index is not an artefact of fitting the large-number tail; it is separate from the grid-resolution check of $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$ above.

The predicted proportion of exact responses shows a small shoulder near $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, even though the fitted width law and sensory-noise profile vary smoothly with $n$ (main-text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:fit}b). The shoulder vanishes when clean-up is switched off at identical parameters (Fig.~\ref{fig:pexact}), so it reflects the dynamics on the integer lattice. Its numerical alignment with $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ does not make the width proxy the point where basins disappear; that question is measured by $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$.

Last, we asked whether the fitted model also reproduces the effective Weber fraction. We computed the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the fitted response distribution, $(1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell/R_{\max}$, per numerosity and exposure (Fig.~\ref{fig:weber}). The CoV is elevated at the smallest numerosities, where the small denominator inflates $\mathrm{sd}/\mathrm{mean}$ in both model and data. It settles to a roughly constant plateau at larger numerosities. For continuity with the original analysis, we fit $w_{\mathrm{eff}}\propto n^\gamma$ over the pre-specified window $n\ge n_{\mathrm{width}}$. This gives a pooled exponent $\gamma=-0.18$ for the model, matching the $-0.16$ measured in the data. Here $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ defines an analysis window, not the onset of an estimation regime.

\section{Supplementary Material 5: Exploratory reanalysis of developmental dyscalculia}
\label{sm:dyscalculia}

As an exploratory extension we applied the winning model to the exact-enumeration data of~\citet{decarli_impaired_2020}: accuracy and response time per subject and numerosity for $32$ children with developmental dyscalculia (DD) and $32$ age-matched controls, using numerosities $n = 1$--$7$ (dropping $n = 8$ exactly as the original authors do, for the guessing end-effect they report). The dot arrays were shown for a fixed exposure of $500$~ms, so all quantities in this section, including $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, refer to that exposure. The model fits both groups well (Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}a,b) and again reproduces the widening well profile of the main dataset. The fitted $n_{\mathrm{width}}\approx8$ falls slightly below the adult values of $9.1$--$9.8$ at $320$--$640$~ms (Supplementary Material~4). This is a comparison of fitted width profiles, not of topological basin counts.

First, the decline of accuracy with numerosity is better explained by changing the fitted well-width profile than by sensory noise. With an exhaustive per-subject grid search and a fixed common lapse and exponent, we fitted two single-mechanism variants to each child's accuracy curve. In the width-only variant, sensory noise is switched off and the decline is carried by broader, more overlapping wells. In the noise-only variant, the width profile is fixed and sensory noise carries the decline. The width-only variant fits better in $64/64$ subjects (mean SSE $0.010$ vs.\ $0.097$, Wilcoxon $p=3.5\times10^{-12}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}c). This comparison localizes the fitted difference to the width profile; it does not establish where attractors disappear.

Second, the group difference falls on the same width parameter. Per subject, $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ is lower in DD (8.1 vs.\ 8.8 for controls; Mann--Whitney $p=0.018$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}}=-0.35$ with the RT channel included; $p=0.044$, $r_{\mathrm{rb}}=-0.29$ in the accuracy-only fits of Fig.~\ref{fig:decarli}d), whereas the mirror analysis that frees sensory noise instead shows only a weak trend ($p=0.05$) and fits worse. In rank terms, the $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ difference is comparable to the raw accuracy deficit at $n=4$--$6$ ($r_{\mathrm{rb}}=-0.33$). This result concerns the continuous width proxy and does not imply a group difference in $n_{\mathrm{topo}}$.

(b)~Median response time, same conventions. The bracket gives the Mann--Whitney $p$ and rank-biserial correlation $r_{\mathrm{rb}}$ for this accuracy-only analysis.}

On this model, then, developmental dyscalculia is modelled as a coarser exact code: the discrete resolution runs out earlier, at the subitizing edge. It is also complementary to the account of~\citet{dolfi_weaker_2024}, who locate the dyscalculic deficit in a weaker number sense within an image-computable network. Both accounts place the deficit in the number code, and coupling the attractor to such a front end (which the present model sets aside) would allow a resolution deficit and a sensory-noise deficit to be separated within a single model. The analysis remains exploratory: the data are coarse ($7$ trials per cell), the localisation to resolution rests on the per-subject model comparison rather than on a full identification of the two mechanisms, and a small sensory-noise component may coexist.
