\begin{abstract}
  
  Numerical perception is often described as the product of two systems: a near-exact mechanism for small sets and an approximate one for larger sets, whose variability follows Weber's law. Gallistel and Gelman proposed instead that both behaviours could arise from a single representation of discrete numerosities \revised{whose precision degrades progressively with magnitude}. \revised{Here we give this idea a mechanistic form, where a noisy estimate of numerosity, already extracted by upstream perceptual processing, is turned into a discrete numerical response.} Integers are represented as memories along a one-dimensional number line, \revised{jointly creating} a Hopfield-inspired attractor landscape. \revised{This estimate} provides the initial state, and recurrent dynamics clean it up by pulling it towards the stored integer representations. Because the memory components become broader and overlap more strongly with magnitude, the same landscape produces near-categorical responses for small numerosities and increasingly coarse, Weber-like estimates for larger ones.

  \medskip

  We tested these predictions by reanalysing 52,995 individual responses from an openly available visual-enumeration dataset. The attractor model outperformed matched static and constant-width controls, a purely driven accumulator, and Weber-style and bounded-optimal alternatives. Response time followed naturally from the number of clean-up steps, with a finite stopping budget accounting for the large-number plateau. The fitted response distributions approached a stable Weber fraction at larger numerosities, and participants with sharper low-number landscapes showed lower Weber noise on a non-overlapping set of large-number trials. Together, these results suggest that magnitude-dependent landscape geometry and recurrent clean-up make separable contributions to a shared numerical readout.
  \end{abstract}

\keywords{numerical cognition, subitizing, Hopfield network, attractor dynamics, approximate number system}

\section{Introduction}
%% ====================================================================

%% Background

% There is OTS and ANS, and nothing in between
Humans, like many non-human animals, possess a non-symbolic ability to extract numerical information from the environment and represent the numerosity of sets, a capacity commonly termed the number sense~\citep{dehaene_number_2011,nieder_neuronal_2016}. This ability is typically expressed in two characteristic behavioural regimes: small sets, typically up to three or four items, are perceived rapidly and with high accuracy, a phenomenon known as subitizing~\citep{kaufman_discrimination_1949, mandler_subitizing_1982}. Beyond this range, responses become slower and less accurate, response variability grows with set size, and discrimination depends primarily on the ratio between numerosities~\citep{feigenson_core_2004,dehaene_number_2011}.
The standard two-systems account attributes these regimes to distinct mechanisms: a capacity-limited Object Tracking System, which individuates a few objects in parallel, and an Approximate Number System, which represents larger numerosities as noisy analogue magnitudes~\citep{feigenson_representations_2002, piazza_subitizing_2011}.

% Model by G&G
An alternative account of the number sense treats both regimes as consequences of a single magnitude code for countable quantities. Gallistel and Gelman proposed that a non-verbal counting process generates analogue magnitudes through successive increments, thereby giving discrete quantities an ordered representation on a linear scale~\citep{gallistel_preverbal_1992,gallistel_non-verbal_2000}. These representations exhibit scalar variability: their standard deviation grows in proportion to magnitude, so neighbouring magnitude distributions overlap increasingly at larger values. Evidence from non-verbal production and rapid sequential-estimation tasks supports this scaling both within and beyond the usual subitizing range~\citep{whalen_nonverbal_1999,cordes_variability_2001}. Increasing overlap could therefore allow one code to support highly accurate small-number judgements while yielding progressively graded, ratio-dependent errors for larger numerosities.

This account describes a general cognitive model of non-verbal number representation, but it does not specify the underlying mechanisms. Their theoretical model alone does not specify how a noisy magnitude is stabilized and mapped back onto an integer response, or whether the exceptional performance observed for the smallest sets is generated by the same process. Gallistel and Gelman themselves left the status of numerosities below five as an open question~\citep{gallistel_non-verbal_2000}. Their proposal therefore provides a representational starting point for a single-code account, while leaving the mechanism of discrete numerical readout unresolved.

% Computational work has shown that it might be only one mechanism
Computational work has developed several single-system explanations of the two behavioural regimes. The central comparison for the present study is the pair of capacity-limited accounts developed by Cheyette, Piantadosi, and colleagues. At a normative level, \citet{cheyette_unified_2020} ask how a system with finite information-processing capacity should encode numerosities under a power-law prior. The resulting bounded-optimal code allocates greater precision to small values and progressively coarser precision to larger ones, producing near-exact small-number performance and Weber-like variability without a change of numerical system. At a more visually explicit level, \citet{cheyette_limited_2024} locate the relevant bottleneck in limits on visuospatial memory, which can generate the characteristic psychophysics of numerosity from visual arrays. Their within-participant analysis found that information-capacity estimates transfer between non-numerical change localisation and numerosity estimation, providing direct evidence for a shared visuospatial constraint. An earlier, representationally distinct model by \citet{zorzi_computational_1999} established a related point: numerical size and distance effects can emerge from a linear cumulative code coupled to nonlinear response competition, without assuming logarithmic compression or scalar representational noise. More recently, \citet{malo_quantum_2023} showed that a small quantum-spin model can encode numerosity in the spectrum of its collective dynamics, providing a distinct dynamical route to a unified numerical representation. Other models have derived numerical sensitivity, range structure, adaptation, and switch-cost phenomena from recurrent visual dynamics~\citep{sengupta_visual_2014,sengupta_big_2017,verma_emergence_2023}, or learned image-computable representations~\citep{stoianov_emergence_2012,testolin_visual_2020}. Together, these accounts show that the two behavioural regimes need not imply two numerical systems, while leaving open whether their common source lies in capacity-limited sensory encoding or in the structure and dynamics of a shared numerical representation.

% This visual mechanisms do not explain the numerical code in non-visual tasks, or accounts of cross-model adaptation (Burr), oar accounts of accuity related to math knowledge, or IPS causal studies
These visual accounts treat numerosity as a product of modality-specific sensory processing, whether as a consequence of limited visuospatial capacity or as a quantity extracted from the image. Number, however, behaves as an abstract quantity that is not bound to any one sense. It transfers across modalities, so that adapting to a stream of sounds or touches shifts the apparent numerosity of a visual array~\citep{arrighi_generalized_2014}, its acuity tracks numerical experience, sharpening with formal schooling in otherwise comparable adults~\citep{piazza_education_2013, nys_does_2013}, and it depends causally on parietal cortex, where stimulating the intraparietal sulcus disrupts numerosity while sparing lower-level vision~\citep{cappelletti_rtms_2007, dormal_contribution_2012} and focal damage yields acalculias that extend even to enumerating touches~\citep{ashkenazi_basic_2008, cohen_tactile_2018}. This picture is not uniform: nonsymbolic number stays robust with little formal schooling once memory and symbolic demands are minimized~\citep{frank_number_2008, zebian_differences_2012, pica_exact_2004}, so this dichotomy is still under debate. This debate motivates the exploration of an architecture in which the classical numerical effects emerge from a purely numerical representation that can be fed by modality-specific sensory processing.

% Alternative account of numerosity coding that is modality agnostic and accounts for both accumulation/summation and neural coding of number
%We thus propose a one-dimensional, Hopfield-inspired energy landscape as this shared numerical stage~\citep{hopfield_neural_1982,ramsauer_hopfield_2021}. Each integer contributes a stored memory component, and these components jointly generate a sequence of attractor basins that becomes broader and shallower with magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}a). We motivate this landscape geometry by the cross-linguistic power-law decline in number-word frequency~\citep{dehaene_cross-linguistic_1992}, so that more frequently encountered numbers form narrow, deep basins, whereas less frequent numbers form broad, shallow ones. A one-dimensional estimate $y_0$ of numerosity, supplied by modality-specific sensory processing, initializes the landscape, followed by recurrent clean-up that stabilizes this evidence through pattern completion (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}b). The attractor therefore models the transformation from noisy numerical evidence to an integer response, while the same dynamics link response time to the number of clean-up steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}c).
We thus propose a one-dimensional, Hopfield-inspired energy landscape as this shared numerical stage~\citep{hopfield_neural_1982,ramsauer_hopfield_2021}. Each integer contributes a stored memory component, and these components jointly generate a sequence of attractor basins that becomes broader and shallower with magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}a). % This explains the two regimes and models G&G
On this view, a single numerical landscape supports two forms of readout. Where neighbouring integer attractors are sufficiently distinct, pattern completion stabilizes noisy evidence around a categorical value, yielding rapid and highly accurate responses. As magnitude increases, adjacent basins broaden, become shallower, and increasingly overlap, so categorical resolution is progressively lost; the same landscape then produces coarser, ratio-dependent estimates. The transition from resolved to approximate responding therefore emerges from the geometry of one representation. The model thus gives mechanistic form to the single-code architecture proposed by Gallistel and Gelman, while adding an explicit representational mechanism: integer-centred attractors embedded in a continuous, noisy state space~\citep{gallistel_preverbal_1992, gallistel_non-verbal_2000}.

% Teaser
We test the model by reanalysing 52,995 individual responses from the openly available brief-exposure dataset of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}. It captures the progression from near-exact small-number reports to underestimation and Weber-like variability at larger numerosities. Model comparisons show that both magnitude-dependent broadening and recurrent clean-up contribute to this fit, while bounded convergence time accounts for the main response-time profile. Finally, across participants, sharper fitted landscapes are associated with lower Weber noise, consistent with a common representational geometry constraining both near-categorical and approximate numerical behaviour.

\section{Methods}
%% ================================================================

We consider the problem of how our model maps a noisy perceptual input onto a discrete numerical response. We posit an energy landscape defined over a continuous axis $x$, with attractor wells associated with integer positions (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}a). In the visual task analysed here, the initial state $y_0$ summarizes evidence extracted from a dot array; formally, the same interface could receive scalar evidence from another sensory modality. Starting from $x = y_0$, the system evolves by gradient descent on the energy landscape until $x$ settles into a stable well, implementing Hopfield-style pattern completion~\citep{hopfield_neural_1982}. The resulting clean-up map $y_0\mapsto x^*$ pulls nearby estimates towards supported integer basins while leaving poorly resolved regions closer to the identity (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}b). A probability distribution over integers is then read out from the final resting state $x^*$. The settling dynamics also determine how long the trial takes.

In our landscape, each integer $n$ acts as a stored memory represented by a normalized Gaussian component centred at $n$. Its scale $\sigma_n$ controls both the breadth and the effective depth of the resulting basin: increasing $\sigma_n$ broadens the component and, through the normalization term $-\log\sigma_n$, lowers its peak contribution to the log-sum-exp landscape. We use the cross-linguistic power-law decline in number-word frequency~\citep{dehaene_cross-linguistic_1992} to motivate this coupled scaling, so frequent small numbers form narrow, deep basins and rarer large numbers form broad, shallow ones. Accordingly, \revised{we assume that} the Gaussian scale grows as a power of magnitude,

%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width}
\sigma_n = \sigma_{c}\, n^{p},
\end{equation}
%
where $\sigma_c$ sets the overall scale and the exponent $p$ controls how fast resolution degrades with magnitude\revised{; both are free parameters fitted to the responses}. Each memory component contributes a log-density
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:well}
g_n(x) = -\log\sigma_n - \frac{(x - n)^2}{2\,\sigma_n^2}.
\end{equation}
%
Using the log-sum-exp energy form associated with modern Hopfield networks~\citep{ramsauer_hopfield_2021}, the attraction potential is the log-sum-exp over all wells, $V(x) = -\log \sum_n \exp\!\big(g_n(x)\big)$.

% Clean up mechanism and bounded steps
A sensory estimate $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(n,\; s_{00}^2 + s_{0}\,n^{\lambda_0}\big)$, whose noise grows with numerosity ($s_{00}=0.05$ fixed; $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ fitted), starts the descent on integer wells at $m=1,\ldots,19$. From $x = y_0$ the state descends the potential, $x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\,\partial V/\partial x$ with fixed $\eta=1$ and a global step clamp (full numerical settings in \hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}). Because $V$ is a log-sum-exp over the stored integers, each step moves the state towards a softmax combination of the wells, weighted by their precisions, so every stored integer contributes to the final position.

The descent stops when the update falls below a fixed tolerance or reverses sign (oscillation). The reversing or sub-threshold update is not applied: $x^*$ is the current state, and $T$ is the recorded stopping-iteration count, including the iteration on which stopping is detected. On the almost flat, large-numerosity landscape, some trajectories do not meet this numerical settling criterion within an iteration budget compatible with the observed response times. We therefore introduce a hypothesized give-up mechanism that stops the clean-up process after $G$ steps, giving $T = \min(T^{*},G)$, where $T^{*}$ is the unbounded settling time. We fix $G$ at the profile-likelihood optimum, $G=120$ (\hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}). A distribution over integer responses is then read out, $p(r \mid x^*) = \mathrm{softmax}_{r}\!\big(g_r(x^*)\big)$, from which we take the mean estimate and the most probable integer.

The model gives a natural interpretation of response time through the time required for the clean-up mechanism to settle on a well:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rt}
\mathrm{RT} = t_{0} + \tau\,\mathbb{E}[T],
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the recorded stopping-iteration count, bounded by the global budget $G$, and the expectation is taken over sensory noise. Observed response times are modelled with a log-normal likelihood for which this value is the median and geometric mean, rather than the arithmetic mean.

To test whether static readout uncertainty contributed beyond the attractor dynamics, we compared this convergence-time link
with an entropy-only alternative, $t_0+\kappa\mathbb{E}[H]$, and with the hybrid $t_0+\tau\mathbb{E}[T]+\kappa\mathbb{E}[H]$. All mapping coefficients were shared across exposure durations, whereas the residual RT variance was duration-specific. The basal latency $t_0=1.30$~s is task-fixed: recorded RT starts at stimulus offset, so $t_0$ absorbs mask, typing, and motor time rather than exposure (\hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}).

We fit the model to the individual responses of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, in which adult participants briefly viewed dot arrays of $1$--$15$ items and typed an estimate, at five exposure durations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640~ms). The source paper analysed four between-experiment arms at $N=100$ each after excluding the ten highest-error participants per arm; the open DURATION file used here pools the three duration-control arms without that exclusion and is within-subject across exposures ($379$ sessions; $360$ completed all five durations). We evaluate the categorical likelihood of the $52{,}995$ individual integer reports. For each numerosity the response probability marginalizes the readout over the sensory noise,
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal}
p(r \mid n) = \int p\big(r \mid x^*(y_0)\big)\,\phi\!\big(y_0; n, \sigma(n)\big)\,dy_0,
\end{equation}
%
with $\sigma(n)=\sqrt{s_{00}^2 + s_0\,n^{\lambda_0}}$, computed by deterministic grid quadrature (\hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}). Typed responses run well beyond the well support, so we add a uniform lapse channel, $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$, with $\ell$ fitted per duration and counted in all information criteria. In the joint fit the landscape $(\sigma_c,p)$ and give-up budget $G$ are shared across durations, while $(s_0,\lambda_0,\ell)$ are duration-specific ($k=18$; \hyperref[sup-sm:comparison]{Supplementary Material~2}), fitted by differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} and Nelder--Mead polishing. With the landscape held fixed, the selected RT channel adds one shared convergence-time coefficient $\tau$ and a per-duration residual $\sigma_{\mathrm{rt}}$. Full numerical settings and fitted values are in \hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}.

We fitted the model with a joint fit in which a single power-law landscape ($\sigma_c$ and $p$) and the give-up budget $G$ were shared across all five exposure durations, while the sensory channel and lapse were allowed to vary by duration. Models were compared under the same trial-level likelihood against a graded set of alternatives. Matched static readouts isolate the contribution of clean-up for both width laws, whereas constant-width controls fix $p=0$ to isolate the contribution of magnitude-dependent broadening (as opposed to the effect of increasing noise variance). A Weber/ANS code and the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020} benchmark the account as a whole. Parameter sharing, total model flexibility, and the corresponding AIC and BIC values are reported in \hyperref[sup-tab:model_comparison]{Supplementary Table~2}.

% Online driven attractor
To test whether the attractor could itself implement numerical accumulation, we fitted a purely driven variant with no completed scalar estimate $y_0$. During exposure, registered items generated a running count $K(t)$, which drove the state while the attractor force acted,
%
\[\tau_x \dot{x}=-\frac{\partial V(x)}{\partial x}+\beta\bigl(K(t)-x\bigr).\]
%
At stimulus offset the drive was removed, and the response was read from the resulting state. This variant tests whether serial evidence accumulation and associative clean-up can be implemented by the same dynamics.

We summarize the fitted width law with the width index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, defined by $\sigma_n=1$, where the fitted Gaussian-component width equals the unit spacing between integers. With $\sigma_n=\sigma_c n^p$, this gives $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$. The definition is equivalent to the heuristic $d'=\mathrm{spacing}/\sigma_n=1$~\citep{macmillan_detection_2004} and summarizes the combined effect of the fitted scale and exponent.

For the individual-differences analysis, all five exposure durations informed two non-overlapping response channels: $n\leq8$ estimated participant effects on lower-numerosity $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, and $n\geq9$ estimated participant effects on Weber noise. Population means were duration-specific, while participant effects on $\log n_{\mathrm{width}}$, $\log w$, and logit lapse were shared across durations and jointly Gaussian. We report the latent $n_{\mathrm{width}}$--$w$ correlation conditional on participant lapse (\hyperref[sup-sm:predictions]{Supplementary Material~4}).

\section{Results}
%% ============================================================

% Our model is the best (of course)
Among the models considered, the power-law attractor provided the best account of the trial-level responses (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}a; \hyperref[sup-sm:comparison]{Supplementary Material~2}). The constant-width attractor trailed by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}\approx3488$, the Weber/ANS code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}\approx838$, and the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020} by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}\approx11{,}453$. The attractor dynamics require one extra "budget" parameter, but, if we switch them off so that we have a static readout of the same wells, the model fits worse by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} \approx 722$. The results thus indicate that the dynamics are an important element of the model, and motivate the introduction of this "give-up" mechanism represented by the budget parameter $G$. Finally, the purely driven accumulator fitted substantially worse than the completed-estimate attractor ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=7954$; Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}a). In this purely driven model, as the running count increased, the state was repeatedly drawn towards intermediate integer representations and often had not reached the presented numerosity by stimulus offset (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sup-fig:puredriven}).

% Response times come out naturally
Because clean-up is a dynamical process, its convergence time provides a direct measure of response time. Adding the entropy term did not improve the fit: the hybrid was 0.24 AIC points worse than convergence time alone, so we retained the simpler model. An entropy-only link was 1579.5 AIC points worse than convergence time (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}b). Therefore, residual readout entropy did not add supported predictive value beyond convergence time. Nevertheless, clean-up on the flat large-numerosity landscape can fail to meet the settling criterion within a behaviourally plausible time budget; allowing every trajectory to reach its eventual limit would then imply implausibly long latencies. The give-up mechanism caps clean-up after $G$ steps, so the predicted response time plateaus with the data once the budget is reached (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}c, vertical line).

% Parameter interpretation: landscape 
The fitted landscape has wells with width growing with magnitude, so they are sharp and well separated for small numbers and broad and overlapping for large ones. A joint parameter-recovery analysis accurately recovers both $\sigma_c$ and $p$, although their estimation errors remain negatively correlated because both determine the same width law (\hyperref[sup-sm:recovery]{Supplementary Material~3}). We therefore summarize their combined effect with the operational index $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$. In the joint fit, the shared width law gives $n_{\mathrm{width}} = 8.5$, which falls near the onset of the response-time plateau (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}c).

% Individual differences
To test whether the same representational geometry constrains lower- and higher-numerosity behaviour across individuals, we related landscape resolution to Weber noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}c). Higher $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ remained associated with lower $w$ after conditioning on the participant lapse effect ($\hat\rho_{nw\cdot\ell}=-.63$, $N=360$; likelihood-ratio $\chi^2(1)=105.0$, $p=1.2\times10^{-24}$). The fitted response distributions also reproduced the Weber-like variability of larger-numerosity estimates, where their coefficient of variation approached a roughly constant plateau with increasing numerosity (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}d). Over $n\geq n_{\mathrm{width}}$, the fitted exponent was shallow in both model and data ($\gamma=-.20$ and $-.10$, respectively), indicating approximately scalar variability across the larger-numerosity range.

% Noise
The sensory channel is only phenomenological: $s_0$ sets the overall noise level of the initial estimate $y_0$, whereas $\lambda_0$ determines how that noise grows with numerosity. These parameters vary across exposure durations so that changes in input quality are not forced onto the shared landscape, and we do not assign them a further cognitive interpretation.

With these ingredients, the model captures all three behavioural measures (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}). Pooled across durations, it gives $R^2=0.95$ for the mean estimate, $0.94$ for exact accuracy, and $0.82$ for response time. The response-time analysis provides a constrained cross-channel test: the landscape is first fitted to the categorical responses and then held fixed, so that only the mapping from convergence time to observed RT is estimated from the response times.

The main deviations are concentrated in the 40-ms condition, particularly in the mean estimates and response times. At such brief exposures, even small numerosities show the graded variability normally associated with larger sets. \citet{cheyette_unified_2020} similarly found that performance at the shortest exposures reflects a severely information-limited regime, and noted that human behaviour changes more sharply with exposure time than predicted by a simple linear accumulation process. The remaining mismatch may therefore reflect early temporal properties of visual encoding that are not captured by our phenomenological sensory channel.

A second residual is a shoulder predicted around $n=6$--$9$ in exact accuracy and $n=5$--$8$ in response time. This structure is generated by the attractor dynamics but is not clearly present in participants' behaviour (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}b,c; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sup-fig:pexact}). We return to its implications for the homogeneity and smoothness of the fitted landscape in the Discussion.

\section{Discussion}
%% ==============================================================

% Summary of results: our model is very cool
We have described a model of the inference of numerosity from noisy scalar evidence as pattern completion on an attractor landscape: the integers act like memory components, made of attractor wells whose widths grow with magnitude, and an uncertain estimate relaxes towards the nearest resolvable integer. Fitted to trial-level visual-enumeration responses, a single landscape shared across exposure durations captures mean estimation, exact accuracy, and the principal response-time profile, while outperforming matched static, constant-width, Weber-style, bounded-optimal, and purely driven alternatives. Within the numerical readout, more categorical and more approximate reports therefore become different operating regions of one representation rather than outputs of two numerical codes joined at an unexplained boundary.

% Geometry and the categorical-to-approximate transition
The magnitude-dependent width profile\revised{, which the trial-level comparison favours over constant-width wells,} explains how this common representation changes its effective resolution. At low numerosities, neighbouring components are narrow and sufficiently isolated for clean-up to concentrate probability on a single integer. As magnitude increases, component overlap grows, exact-response probability declines, and the resulting response distributions approach the roughly constant coefficient of variation characteristic of nonsymbolic estimation~\citep{whalen_nonverbal_1999,cordes_variability_2001,testolin_estimates_2021}. The main residual of this account is a shoulder around $n=5$--$9$ generated by clean-up on the integer lattice but less evident in the behavioural data. The empirical transition may therefore be smoother than in the fitted homogeneous landscape, potentially because participants differ in landscape geometry or in when they stop deliberating. The fitted power-law widening is defined on a linear coordinate, but the present data do not uniquely identify this geometry: an appropriately compressed coordinate with a different width rule could provide a related description.

% What clean-up adds, and what it does not do
The matched static control shows that the attractor dynamics contribute beyond the width profile itself. In the static model, sensory variability passes directly into the readout, whereas clean-up instead maps nearby estimates that fall within the same basin towards a common final state before the response is generated. Where integer components are well separated, this compresses the variability of the input: a broader range of noisy estimates yields a readout concentrated around the same integer, increasing exact-response probability and narrowing the distribution of reports. As neighbouring components broaden and overlap, the basins become less selective and this stabilizing effect weakens. The Hopfield analogy nevertheless has limits: the purely driven accumulator fails to capture the data, implying that accumulation and numerosity retrieval are separate processes. This result is understandable because a partial count is not a degraded representation of the final cardinality: each prefix is itself a valid numerosity and remains compatible with every larger total until the set or sequence is complete. The attractor therefore cannot recover the final number from the running count alone. In the present architecture, modality-specific processes first construct an estimate of a delimited set or completed sequence, after which the shared landscape stabilizes that estimate.

% Response time and finite stopping
The chronometric result provides a second consequence of the same dynamics. Under a single latent-to-time mapping shared across exposure durations, convergence time accounts for response time as well as the hybrid convergence-plus-entropy link and substantially better than residual entropy alone. The selected model therefore ties latency directly to the amount of attractor clean-up required\revised{, similarly to \citet{zorzi_computational_1999}.} In the flat large-numerosity region, however, full convergence can require more iterations than a behaviourally plausible response process would allow. We therefore introduce a simple ``give-up'' mechanism that truncates clean-up after a maximum of $G$ steps. This converts the otherwise continuing increase in settling time into the observed response-time plateau. The plateau thus reflects the interaction between slowing attractor dynamics and a finite stopping policy: the present model captures the behavioural effect of that policy, while leaving its cognitive implementation open.

% Individual differences, experience, and development
The width profile provides a potential representational locus for individual and developmental differences in numerical acuity. In the present data, participants with a sharper fitted landscape at lower numerosities also show less Weber noise on a non-overlapping set of larger-numerosity trials. This association is consistent with a common geometry constraining both categorical and approximate behaviour, although the evidence remains correlational. The memory-like integer components, and this link with individual Weber noise, imply that one possible origin of differences in numerical acuity (and thus mathematics achievement~\citep{halberda_individual_2008}) is numerical experience: if memory precision adapts to environmental statistics, frequently encountered \revised{numerosities} should be represented more sharply than rarer ones~\citep{anderson_reflections_1991,verguts_representation_2004}. \revised{This experience need not be symbolic or scholastic: small numerosities dominate everyday environments across modalities and formats, so the relevant statistics are available to unschooled humans and to other animals, in which numerosity tuning arises without numerical training~\citep{viswanathan_neuronal_2013}. On this view, development} and numerical training should then progressively modify the width profile and improve acuity. \revised{This prediction concerns experience with \emph{numerosities}:} an intervention that sharpens the numerical landscape should produce different transfer from one that improves broader visuospatial perception~\citep{butterworth_investigating_2017}.

% Integers as memories is cool
At the same time, the memory analogy has an important constraint. Integer memories are not independent patterns that can be strengthened arbitrarily: they occupy ordered positions in a one-dimensional Hopfield landscape, so changing one representation may alter neighbouring wells, basin boundaries, and the global resolution profile. This structure makes targeted training---and potentially the remediation of some number-sense impairments---an attractive possibility, but it also leaves open how the landscape could be sharpened as a whole without distorting its numerical organization. 

% Why the model omits a sensory front end
The omission of a sensory front end is deliberate because the present study was designed to isolate the computations performed after scalar numerical evidence has become available. This separation matters because learned visual systems can already extract numerosity-selective features from unlabelled pixel-level input: \citet{stoianov_emergence_2012} showed that such representations can emerge through unsupervised visual learning, and subsequent image-computable models reproduce effects of continuous visual magnitudes and dyscalculic profiles~\citep{testolin_visual_2020,dolfi_weaker_2024}. Adding a visual extractor to the present first test would therefore make it difficult to determine whether predictive success arose from sensory feature construction or from numerical pattern completion. Isolating the attractor avoids that ambiguity, but also defines the model's scope: it does not explain segmentation, continuous image statistics, spatial configuration, attentional load, or other perceptual contributions to classical subitizing effects. \revised{Evidence that attention selectively supports performance in the subitizing range, from dual-task costs~\citep{burr_subitizing_2010} to the loss of the small-number advantage in speeded saccadic choices~\citep{castaldi_fast_2020}, fits this division of labour: on the present account, such manipulations degrade the evidence delivered to the landscape, much as brief exposure does in the data modelled here.} A complete perceptual account should couple the landscape to an image-computable or recurrent visual front end and test whether attractor clean-up predicts structure beyond the front end's own readout. Parallel auditory and tactile implementations would then provide a direct test of whether modality-specific sensory channels converge on a common numerical landscape. Providing a sensory input to the current model is a natural direction for future work.

% Closing
Numerical readout, in this framework, is pattern completion on a landscape of memories of the integer numbers. Categorical and approximate reports become two readings of one landscape rather than two codes joined at an unexplained seam. The bounded convergence time of the clean-up dynamics captures the main response-time profile, although the cognitive implementation of the stopping policy remains open. The result is a compact, mechanistic set of hypotheses about how the mind represents number.
