\begin{abstract}
  Numerosity judgements are often attributed to separate mechanisms for near-exact small-set and approximate large-set perception. We test a single-representation alternative in which integer-centred memory components form an attractor landscape with a power-law width profile. A noisy scalar estimate initializes the landscape, and recurrent pattern completion maps it onto a distribution over integer responses. Increasing overlap among components makes the same representation yield progressively coarser, Weber-like reports rather than switching to a separate approximate code. Fitted to 52,995 visual-enumeration responses across five exposure durations, the shared-landscape model outperforms matched static and constant-width controls, a purely driven accumulator, and Weber-style and bounded-optimal alternatives. Recurrent clean-up improves response prediction, and bounded convergence time captures the main response-time profile. A hierarchical analysis links small-number landscape resolution to large-number Weber noise across participants. Together, the results isolate magnitude-dependent geometry and recurrent clean-up as separable contributors to numerical readout.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{numerical cognition, subitizing, exact number system, attractor dynamics, approximate number system}

\section{Introduction}
%% ====================================================================

%% Background

% There is OTS and ANS, and nothing in between
Humans, like many non-human animals, possess a non-symbolic ability to extract numerical information from the environment and represent the numerosity of sets, a capacity commonly termed the number sense~\citep{dehaene_number_2011,nieder_neuronal_2016}. This ability is typically expressed in two characteristic behavioural regimes: small sets, typically up to three or four items, are apprehended rapidly and with high accuracy, a phenomenon known as subitizing~\citep{kaufman_discrimination_1949, mandler_subitizing_1982}. Beyond this range, responses become slower and less accurate, response variability grows with set size, and discrimination depends primarily on the ratio between numerosities~\citep{feigenson_core_2004,dehaene_number_2011}. The standard two-systems account attributes these regimes to distinct mechanisms: a capacity-limited Object Tracking System, which individuates a few objects in parallel, and an Approximate Number System, which represents larger numerosities as noisy analogue magnitudes~\citep{feigenson_representations_2002, piazza_subitizing_2011}. 

% Model by G&G
An alternative account of the number sense treats both regimes as outputs of a single code for discrete numerosities. Gallistel and Gelman proposed that a non-verbal counting process represents discrete, countable quantities as analogue magnitudes generated by successive increments and arranged on a linear scale~\citep{gallistel_preverbal_1992,gallistel_non-verbal_2000}. These representations exhibit scalar variability: their standard deviation grows in proportion to magnitude, so neighbouring magnitude distributions overlap increasingly at larger values. This provides a route by which one code can support highly accurate small-number judgements while yielding increasingly graded, ratio-dependent errors for larger numerosities, without assigning the two regimes to separate systems~\citep{whalen_nonverbal_1999, cordes_variability_2001}.

% Computational work has shown that it might be only one mechanism
Computational work has developed several single-system explanations of the two behavioural regimes. The central comparison for the present study is the pair of capacity-limited accounts developed by Cheyette, Piantadosi, and colleagues. At a normative level, \citet{cheyette_unified_2020} ask how a system with finite information-processing capacity should encode numerosities under a power-law prior. The resulting bounded-optimal code allocates greater precision to small values and progressively coarser precision to larger ones, producing near-exact small-number performance and Weber-like variability without a change of numerical system. At a more visually explicit level, \citet{cheyette_limited_2024} locate the relevant bottleneck in limits on visuospatial memory, which can generate the characteristic psychophysics of numerosity from visual arrays. An earlier, representationally distinct model by \citet{zorzi_computational_1999} established a related point: numerical size and distance effects can emerge from a linear cumulative code coupled to nonlinear response competition, without assuming logarithmic compression or scalar representational noise. More recently, \citet{malo_quantum_2023} showed that a small quantum-spin model can encode numerosity in the spectrum of its collective dynamics, providing a distinct dynamical route to a unified numerical representation. Other models have derived numerical sensitivity from recurrent visual dynamics~\citep{sengupta_visual_2014,sengupta_big_2017} or learned image-computable representations~\citep{stoianov_emergence_2012,testolin_visual_2020}. Together, these accounts show that the two behavioural regimes need not imply two numerical systems, while leaving open whether their common source lies in capacity-limited sensory encoding or in the structure and dynamics of a shared numerical representation.

% This visual mechanisms do not explain the numerical code in non-visual tasks, or accounts of cross-model adaptation (Burr), oar accounts of accuity related to math knowledge, or IPS causal studies
These visual accounts treat numerosity as a product of modality-specific sensory processing, whether as a consequence of limited visuospatial capacity or as a quantity extracted from the image. Number, however, behaves as an abstract quantity that is not bound to any one sense. It transfers across modalities, so that adapting to a stream of sounds or touches shifts the apparent numerosity of a visual array~\citep{arrighi_generalized_2014}, its acuity tracks numerical experience, sharpening with formal schooling in otherwise comparable adults~\citep{piazza_education_2013, nys_does_2013}, and it depends causally on parietal cortex, where stimulating the intraparietal sulcus disrupts numerosity while sparing lower-level vision~\citep{cappelletti_rtms_2007, dormal_contribution_2012} and focal damage yields acalculias that extend even to enumerating touches~\citep{ashkenazi_basic_2008, cohen_tactile_2018}. This picture is not uniform: nonsymbolic number stays robust with little formal schooling once memory and symbolic demands are minimized~\citep{frank_number_2008, zebian_differences_2012, pica_exact_2004}, so this dichotomy is still under debate. Together, these findings motivate an architecture in which modality-specific sensory processing feeds a shared numerical representation.

% Alternative account of numerosity coding that is modality agnostic and accounts for both accumulation/summation and neural coding of number
We propose a one-dimensional, Hopfield-inspired energy landscape as this shared numerical stage~\citep{hopfield_neural_1982,ramsauer_hopfield_2021}. Each integer contributes a stored memory component, and these components jointly generate a sequence of attractor basins that becomes broader and shallower with magnitude. We motivate this landscape geometry by the cross-linguistic power-law decline in number-word frequency~\citep{dehaene_cross-linguistic_1992}, so that more frequently encountered numbers form narrow, deep basins, whereas less frequent numbers form broad, shallow ones. A one-dimensional estimate $y_0$ of numerosity, supplied by modality-specific sensory processing, initializes the landscape, followed by recurrent clean-up that stabilizes this evidence through pattern completion. The attractor therefore models the transformation from noisy numerical evidence to an integer response, while the same dynamics link response time to settling time.

% This explains the two regimes and models G&G
On this view, a single numerical landscape supports two forms of readout. Where neighbouring integer attractors are sufficiently distinct, pattern completion stabilizes noisy evidence around a categorical value, yielding rapid and highly accurate responses. As magnitude increases, adjacent basins broaden, become shallower, and increasingly overlap, so categorical resolution is progressively lost; the same landscape then produces coarser, ratio-dependent estimates. The transition from resolved to approximate responding is therefore not implemented as a switch between numerical systems, but emerges from the geometry of one representation. The model thus gives mechanistic form to the single-code architecture proposed by Gallistel and Gelman, while adding an explicit representational mechanism: integer-centred attractors embedded in a continuous, noisy state space~\citep{gallistel_preverbal_1992, gallistel_non-verbal_2000}.

% Teaser
We test this account on ${\sim}$53,000 individual responses from the brief-exposure dataset of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, spanning five presentation durations from 40 to 640~ms. It reproduces both the underestimation of larger numerosities and the fall-off of exact accuracy with set size, and outperforms a Weber/ANS baseline and their own bounded-optimal model. Turning on the clean-up dynamics improves the fit at the cost of a single shared give-up budget, while the attractor's settling time predicts response times, whose plateau at larger numerosities finds no natural account in the model without that budget. At larger numerosities, the fitted response distributions recover Weber's law, with an effective Weber fraction closely matching the data. In an exploratory reanalysis of developmental dyscalculia, the model further links poorer exact enumeration to a coarser width profile rather than increased sensory noise.

\section{Methods}
%% ================================================================

We thus consider the problem of how our model maps a noisy perceptual input onto a discrete numerical response. We posit an energy landscape defined over a continuous axis $x$, with attractor wells associated with integer positions (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}a). In the visual task analysed here, the initial state $y_0$ summarizes evidence extracted from a dot array; formally, the same interface could receive scalar evidence from another sensory modality. Starting from $x = y_0$, the system evolves by gradient descent on the energy landscape until $x$ settles into a stable well, implementing Hopfield-style pattern completion~\citep{hopfield_neural_1982}. A probability distribution over integers is then read out from the final resting state $x^*$. The settling dynamics also determine how long the trial takes.

Figure~\ref{fig:comparison}c reports the width index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$. \textbf{b}, Expected readout entropy $E[H]$ per presented numerosity, before (grey) and after (black) clean-up, with the attractor's expected convergence time $E[T]$ (blue, right axis); insets show example post-clean-up posteriors. Clean-up removes the residual uncertainty of the small-$n$ readout at the cost of a few settling steps; near the boundary the settling time grows and the residual entropy rises. \textbf{c}, The selected response-time link, $\mathrm{RT} = t_0 + \tau\,E[T]$, decomposed into basal latency and convergence time.}

In our landscape, each integer $n$ acts as a stored memory represented by a normalized Gaussian component centred at $n$. Its scale $\sigma_n$ controls both the breadth and the effective depth of the resulting basin: increasing $\sigma_n$ broadens the component and, through the normalization term $-\log\sigma_n$, lowers its peak contribution to the log-sum-exp landscape. We use the cross-linguistic power-law decline in number-word frequency~\citep{dehaene_cross-linguistic_1992} to motivate this coupled scaling, so frequent small numbers form narrow, deep basins and rarer large numbers form broad, shallow ones. Accordingly, the Gaussian scale grows as a power of magnitude,

%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width}
\sigma_n = \sigma_{c}\, n^{p},
\end{equation}
%
where $\sigma_c$ sets the overall scale and the exponent $p$ controls how fast resolution degrades with magnitude. Each memory component contributes a log-density
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:well}
g_n(x) = -\log\sigma_n - \frac{(x - n)^2}{2\,\sigma_n^2}.
\end{equation}
%
Using the log-sum-exp energy form associated with modern Hopfield networks~\citep{ramsauer_hopfield_2021}, the attraction potential is the log-sum-exp over all wells, $V(x) = -\log \sum_n \exp\!\big(g_n(x)\big)$.

% Clean up mechanism and bounded steps
A sensory estimate $y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(n,\; s_{00}^2 + s_{0}\,n^{\lambda_0}\big)$, whose noise grows with numerosity ($s_{00}=0.05$ fixed; $s_0$ and $\lambda_0$ fitted), starts the descent on integer wells at $m=1,\ldots,19$. From $x = y_0$ the state descends the potential, $x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\,\partial V/\partial x$ with fixed $\eta=1$ and a global step clamp (full numerical settings in \hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}). Because $V$ is a log-sum-exp over the stored integers, each step moves the state towards a softmax combination of the wells, weighted by their precisions, so every stored integer contributes to the final position. The descent stops when the update falls below a fixed tolerance or reverses sign (oscillation); $x^*$ is then the current state and $T$ the iteration at stopping. On the almost flat, large-numerosity landscape the state does not settle within a single well, so the clean-up never converges and would run indefinitely. Therefore, we introduce a hypothesized give-up mechanism that stops the clean-up process after $G$ steps, giving $T = \min(T^{*},G)$, where $T^{*}$ is the unbounded settling time. We fix $G$ at the profile-likelihood optimum, $G=120$ (\hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}). A distribution over integers is then read out, $p(n \mid x^*) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\big(g_n(x^*)\big)$, from which we take the mean estimate and the most probable integer.

The model gives a natural interpretation of response time through the time required for the clean-up mechanism to settle on a well:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rt}
\mathrm{RT} = t_{0} + \tau\,\mathbb{E}[T],
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the number of clean-up steps, bounded by the global budget $G$, and the expectation is taken over sensory noise. Observed response times are modelled with a log-normal likelihood centred on this value. To test whether static readout uncertainty contributed beyond the attractor dynamics, we compared this convergence-time link
with an entropy-only alternative, $t_0+\kappa\mathbb{E}[H]$, and with the hybrid $t_0+\tau\mathbb{E}[T]+\kappa\mathbb{E}[H]$. All mapping coefficients were shared across exposure durations, whereas the residual RT variance was duration-specific. The basal latency $t_0=1.30$~s is task-fixed: recorded RT starts at stimulus offset, so $t_0$ absorbs mask, typing, and motor time rather than exposure (\hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}).

We fit the model to the individual responses of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}, in which adult participants briefly viewed dot arrays of $1$--$15$ items and typed an estimate, at five exposure durations (40, 80, 160, 320, 640~ms). The source paper analysed four between-experiment arms at $N=100$ each after excluding the ten highest-error participants per arm; the open DURATION file used here pools the three duration-control arms without that exclusion and is within-subject across exposures ($379$ sessions; $360$ completed all five durations). We evaluate the categorical likelihood of the $52{,}995$ individual integer reports. For each numerosity the response probability marginalizes the readout over the sensory noise,
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal}
p(r \mid n) = \int p\big(r \mid x^*(y_0)\big)\,\phi\!\big(y_0; n, \sigma(n)\big)\,dy_0,
\end{equation}
%
with $\sigma(n)=\sqrt{s_{00}^2 + s_0\,n^{\lambda_0}}$, computed by deterministic grid quadrature (\hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}). Typed responses run well beyond the well support, so we add a uniform lapse channel, $p_{\mathrm{final}}(r \mid n) = (1-\ell)\,p(r \mid n) + \ell\,/\,R_{\max}$, with $\ell$ fitted per duration and counted in all information criteria. In the joint fit the landscape $(\sigma_c,p)$ and give-up budget $G$ are shared across durations, while $(s_0,\lambda_0,\ell)$ are duration-specific ($k=18$; \hyperref[sup-sm:comparison]{Supplementary Material~2}), fitted by differential evolution~\citep{storn_differential_1997} and Nelder--Mead polishing. With the landscape held fixed, the selected RT channel adds one shared convergence-time coefficient $\tau$ and a per-duration residual $\sigma_{\mathrm{rt}}$. Full numerical settings and fitted values are in \hyperref[sup-sm:methods]{Supplementary Material~1}.

We fitted the model with a joint fit in which a single power-law landscape ($\sigma_c$ and $p$) and the give-up budget $G$ were shared across all five exposure durations, while the sensory channel and lapse were allowed to vary by duration. Models were compared under the same trial-level likelihood against a graded set of alternatives. Matched static readouts isolate the contribution of clean-up for both width laws, whereas constant-width controls fix $p=0$ to isolate the contribution of magnitude-dependent broadening (as opposed to the effect of increasing noise variance). A Weber/ANS code and the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020} benchmark the account as a whole. Parameter sharing, total model flexibility, and the corresponding AIC and BIC values are reported in \hyperref[sup-tab:model_comparison]{Supplementary Table~2}.

% Online driven attractor
To test whether the attractor could itself implement numerical accumulation, we fitted a purely driven variant with no completed scalar estimate $y_0$. During exposure, registered items generated a running count $K(t)$, which drove the state while the attractor force acted,
%
\[\tau_x \dot{x}=-\frac{\partial V(x)}{\partial x}+\beta\bigl(K(t)-x\bigr).\]
%
At stimulus offset the drive was removed, and the response was read from the resulting state. This variant tests whether serial evidence accumulation and associative clean-up can be implemented by the same dynamics.

We summarize the fitted width law with the width index $n_{\mathrm{width}}$, defined by $\sigma_n=1$, where the fitted Gaussian-component width equals the unit spacing between integers. With $\sigma_n=\sigma_c n^p$, this gives $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$. The definition is equivalent to the heuristic $d'=\mathrm{spacing}/\sigma_n=1$~\citep{macmillan_detection_2004} and summarizes the combined effect of the fitted scale and exponent.

Finally, as an exploratory application, we fit the same model to the exact-enumeration data of~\citet{decarli_impaired_2020} from children with and without developmental dyscalculia, localizing the group difference within the landscape (\hyperref[sup-sm:dyscalculia]{Supplementary Material~5}).

\section{Results}
%% ============================================================

% Our model is the best (of course)
Among the models considered, the power-law attractor provided the best account of the trial-level responses (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}a; \hyperref[sup-sm:comparison]{Supplementary Material~2}). The constant-width attractor trailed by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}\approx3488$, the Weber/ANS code by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}\approx838$, and the bounded-optimal model of~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020} by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}\approx11{,}453$. We also analyse the effect of the attractor dynamics on model performance. If we switch them off, so that we have a static readout of the same wells with one parameter fewer (it needs no budget), the model fits worse by $\Delta\mathrm{AIC} \approx 722$ under the joint fit. The dynamics therefore sharpen the response distribution at the cost of a single global budget parameter. Finally, the purely driven accumulator fitted substantially worse than the completed-estimate attractor ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=7954$; Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}a). In this purely driven model, as the running count increased, the state was repeatedly drawn towards intermediate integer representations and often had not reached the presented numerosity by stimulus offset (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sup-fig:puredriven}).

% Response times come out naturally
Because clean-up is a dynamical process, its convergence time provides a direct predictor of response time. The convergence-only and hybrid links were effectively indistinguishable ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=0.24$), so we retained convergence time alone as the more parsimonious model. The entropy-only link performed substantially worse ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=1579.5$; Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}b). Thus, under a single latent-to-time mapping shared across exposure durations, residual readout entropy did not add supported predictive value beyond convergence time. Nevertheless, since clean-up does not converge on the flat large-numerosity landscape, its settling time would grow without bound; the give-up mechanism caps it after $G$ steps, so the predicted response time plateaus onto the data rather than diverging past the budget's onset (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}c, vertical line).

% Parameter interpretation: landscape 
The fitted landscape has wells with width growing with magnitude, so they are sharp and well separated for small numbers and broad and overlapping for large ones. Since $\sigma_c$ and $p$ are not identifiable under a parameter recovery analysis (\hyperref[sup-sm:recovery]{Supplementary Material~3}), we summarize the fitted width law with the operational index $n_{\mathrm{width}}=\sigma_c^{-1/p}$, which combines its scale and exponent into a single profile-level measure. In the joint fit, the shared width law gives $n_{\mathrm{width}} = 8.5$, which coincides with the onset of the response-time plateau (Fig.~\ref{fig:conceptual}c).

% Individual differences
To test whether the same representational geometry constrains lower- and higher-numerosity behaviour across individuals, we related landscape resolution to Weber noise using non-overlapping sets of trials. Higher $n_{\mathrm{width}}$ was associated with lower $w$: the bivariate hierarchical model estimated a latent participant correlation of $\hat{\rho}=-.57$ ($N=362$; likelihood-ratio $\chi^2(1)=156.9$, $p<10^{-35}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}c), robust to neighbouring definitions of the two numerosity ranges (\hyperref[sup-sm:predictions]{Supplementary Material~4}). The fitted response distributions also reproduced the Weber-like variability of larger-numerosity estimates. Their coefficient of variation approached a roughly constant plateau with increasing numerosity, as did the empirical distributions (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}d). Over $n\geq n_{\mathrm{width}}$, the fitted exponent was shallow in both model and data ($\gamma=-.20$ and $-.10$, respectively), indicating approximately scalar variability across the larger-numerosity range.

% Noise
The sensory channel is phenomenological: $s_0$ sets the overall noise level of the initial estimate $y_0$, whereas $\lambda_0$ determines how that noise grows with numerosity. These parameters vary across exposure durations so that changes in input quality are not forced onto the shared landscape, and we do not assign them a further cognitive interpretation.

With these ingredients the model reproduces all three behavioural signatures together (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}): pooled across durations, $R^2 = 0.95$ for the mean estimate, $0.94$ for exact accuracy, and $0.82$ for response time, the last obtained from a constrained cross-channel fit in which the landscape was held fixed at its response-fit values and only the RT mapping was estimated from response times. There are two systematic deviations in the model predictions: the first is the 40-ms condition overall, which accumulates many deviations from the predictions, similarly to~\citet{cheyette_unified_2020}. The other is a predicted shoulder around $n=6$--$9$ in the accuracy data (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}b), and around $n=5$--$8$ in the response time data (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}c), which are caused by the attractor dynamics (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sup-fig:pexact}), and not observed in the participants' behaviour.

Finally, we explored how the model would account for developmental dyscalculia by applying it to data on numerosity extraction of dyscalculic children versus age-matched controls from~\citet{decarli_impaired_2020}. The fitted landscapes of children with dyscalculia contained broader, more overlapping components and yielded a lower extrapolated width index (median $n_{\mathrm{width}}=8.1$ vs.\ 8.8; Mann--Whitney $p=.018$), consistent with a coarser fitted width profile (see \hyperref[sup-sm:dyscalculia]{Supplementary Material~5}; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{sup-fig:decarli}).

\section{Discussion}
%% ==============================================================

% Summary of results: our model is very cool
We have described the inference of numerosity from noisy scalar evidence as pattern completion on an attractor landscape: the integers act like memories, made of attractor wells whose widths grow with magnitude, and an uncertain estimate relaxes towards the nearest resolvable region. Fitted to trial-level visual-enumeration responses, a single landscape shared across exposure durations captures mean estimation, exact accuracy, and the principal response-time profile, while outperforming matched static, constant-width, Weber-style, bounded-optimal, and purely driven alternatives. Within the numerical readout, more categorical and more approximate reports therefore become different operating regions of one representation rather than outputs of two numerical codes joined at an unexplained boundary.

% Geometry and the categorical-to-approximate transition
The magnitude-dependent width profile explains how this common representation changes its effective resolution. At low numerosities, neighbouring components are narrow and sufficiently isolated for clean-up to concentrate probability on a single integer. As magnitude increases, component overlap grows, exact-response probability declines, and the resulting response distributions approach the roughly constant coefficient of variation characteristic of nonsymbolic estimation~\citep{whalen_nonverbal_1999,cordes_variability_2001,testolin_estimates_2021}. The main residual of this account is a shoulder around $n=5$--$9$ generated by clean-up on the integer lattice but less evident in the behavioural data. The empirical transition may therefore be smoother than in the fitted homogeneous landscape, potentially because participants differ in landscape geometry or in when they stop deliberating.

% What clean-up adds, and what it does not do
The matched static control shows that the attractor dynamics contribute beyond the width profile itself. Clean-up implements a Hopfield-style associative-retrieval operation: each update is determined by competition among the stored integers, so nearby inputs are pulled towards regions supported by the landscape and the categorical response distribution is sharpened. But the Hopfield analogy has its limits, since the purely driven accumulator fails at correctly capturing the data, implying that accumulation and numerosity retrieval are two separate processes. And this is not surprising, since a partial count is not a degraded representation of the final cardinality: each prefix is itself a valid numerosity and remains compatible with every larger total until the set or sequence is complete. The attractor therefore cannot recover the final number from the running count alone. In the present architecture, modality-specific processes first construct an estimate of a delimited set or completed sequence, after which the shared landscape stabilizes that estimate. 

% Response time and finite stopping
The chronometric result provides a second consequence of the same dynamics. Under a single latent-to-time mapping shared across exposure durations, convergence time accounts for response time as well as the hybrid convergence-plus-entropy link and substantially better than residual entropy alone. The selected model therefore ties latency to the amount of attractor clean-up rather than to a generic static uncertainty measure. On the flat large-numerosity region, however, unconstrained settling time would continue to increase. The global budget $G$ converts this slowing into the observed response-time plateau, so the plateau reflects an interaction between attractor dynamics and a finite stopping policy. The model captures the behavioural consequence of that policy, but its cognitive implementation remains open.

% Individual differences, experience, and development
The width profile provides a potential representational locus for individual and developmental differences in numerical acuity. In the present data, participants with a sharper fitted landscape at lower numerosities also show less Weber noise on a non-overlapping set of larger-numerosity trials, consistent with a common geometry constraining both categorical and approximate behaviour. One possible origin of these differences is numerical experience: if memory precision adapts to environmental statistics, frequently encountered numbers should be represented more sharply than rarer ones~\citep{anderson_reflections_1991,verguts_representation_2004}. Development and numerical training should then progressively modify the width profile and improve acuity. Crucially, this prediction concerns experience with \emph{numbers}, rather than visual improvement in general: an intervention that sharpens the numerical landscape should produce different transfer from one that improves broader visuospatial perception~\citep{butterworth_investigating_2017}.

% Integers as memories is cool
At the same time, the memory analogy has an important constraint. Integer memories are not independent patterns that can be strengthened arbitrarily: they occupy ordered positions in a one-dimensional Hopfield landscape, so changing one representation may alter neighbouring wells, basin boundaries, and the global resolution profile. This structure makes targeted training---and potentially the remediation of some number-sense impairments---an attractive possibility, but it also leaves open how the landscape could be sharpened as a whole without distorting its numerical organization. The exploratory dyscalculia analysis provides an initial illustration of this framework, with the affected group showing a coarser fitted width profile, but it does not establish how that difference arose or whether it is trainable.

% Why the model omits a sensory front end
The omission of a sensory front end is deliberate because the present study was designed to isolate the computations performed after scalar numerical evidence has become available. This separation matters because learned visual systems can already extract numerosity-selective features from unlabelled pixel-level input: \citet{stoianov_emergence_2012} showed that such representations can emerge through unsupervised visual learning, and subsequent image-computable models reproduce effects of continuous visual magnitudes and dyscalculic profiles~\citep{testolin_visual_2020,dolfi_weaker_2024}. Adding a visual extractor to the present first test would therefore make it difficult to determine whether predictive success arose from sensory feature construction or from numerical pattern completion. Isolating the attractor avoids that ambiguity, but also defines the model's scope: it does not explain segmentation, continuous image statistics, spatial configuration, attentional load, or other perceptual contributions to classical subitizing effects. A complete perceptual account should couple the landscape to an image-computable or recurrent visual front end and test whether attractor clean-up predicts structure beyond the front end's own readout. Parallel auditory and tactile implementations would then provide a direct test of whether modality-specific sensory channels converge on a common numerical landscape.

% Closing
Numerical readout, in this framework, is pattern completion on a landscape of number memories. Within the numerical readout, more categorical and approximate reports become two readings of one landscape rather than two codes joined at an unexplained seam. The bounded convergence time of the clean-up dynamics also captures the main response-time profile, although the cognitive implementation of the stopping policy remains open. The result is a compact, mechanistic set of hypotheses about how the mind represents number.
